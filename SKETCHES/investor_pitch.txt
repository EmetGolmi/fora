Mr. Buffett, thank you for your time.

Civic participation in America is broken — not because people don't care, but because the information is scattered, inaccessible, and designed for insiders. The average citizen can't tell you who represents them at the state level, what those officials actually voted for, or who paid for their campaigns. That ignorance is expensive — it costs us in bad policy, low turnout, and a widening gap between the governed and the governing.

FORA solves that. It's a platform where any citizen enters their address and immediately sees every official who represents them — federal, state, and local — their voting records, their sponsored legislation, their campaign donors, and their committee assignments, explained in plain English. Then, alongside that, they see the bills that actually affect their neighborhood, moving through government in real time.

Now — how do we make money.

Three streams. First, a subscription tier for power users — journalists, researchers, civic organizations, and engaged citizens who want deeper data, export tools, and tracking. Think $10 to $15 a month. Second, verified official accounts. We become the place where elected officials communicate directly with constituents — not through Twitter, not through a press release, but through a platform designed for accountability. Officials pay for verification and enhanced communication tools, the same way businesses pay for LinkedIn Premium. Third, government and institutional contracts. School districts, civic leagues, bar associations, unions — organizations that need their members civically informed will license FORA the way they license any professional tool.

Who are our real competitors?

Not Ballotpedia — they're a reference encyclopedia, not a platform. Not GovTrack — they're a tool for legislative staff, not citizens. Our real competitor is apathy — the decision a person makes every morning not to engage because the information isn't worth the effort to find. The second real competitor is social media, which has colonized the civic conversation and turned it into outrage rather than participation. We're not building a news feed. We're building infrastructure.

What does this industry look like in ten years?

Governments worldwide are under pressure to digitize constituent engagement. The question isn't whether civic technology will scale — it's who builds the layer that citizens actually trust. Right now there's no dominant platform. The space looks the way social media looked in 2003, the way professional networking looked before LinkedIn, the way local commerce looked before anyone built a reliable directory. The winner will be whoever builds the habit first — the place people go automatically when a bill passes, when an election approaches, when they want to contact their representative.

FORA is built on four pillars that must all exist together or none of them work. Identity — who you are as a citizen, your values and your place in your community. Knowledge — the law, the discourse, the verified record. Commerce — the contracts, the permits, the economic life of a city. And governance — the officials, the votes, the legislation. Separate any one of them and you have a vertical app. Together, they are a digital twin of society.

We're starting in Philadelphia. We're already live. The data is real, the officials are real, and people are using it today.

We're not asking you to bet on an idea. We're asking you to look at what we've already built and tell us where you see the ceiling.
