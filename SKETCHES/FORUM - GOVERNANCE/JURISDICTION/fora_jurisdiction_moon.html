Overview
The most contested body without a law

The Moon is 384,400 kilometers away, has no atmosphere, no liquid water on its surface, and temperatures that swing from 127°C at lunar noon to −173°C at night. It has also been, since 1969, one of only two extraterrestrial bodies ever visited by humans — and it is about to receive humans again for the first time in over half a century.

Artemis II launches April 1, 2026 — a 10-day crewed flyby, the first time humans will reach the Moon's vicinity since Apollo 17 landed in December 1972. It carries no legal settlement to the questions the Moon now raises: who can use its resources, who can establish permanent bases, and how two competing great-power programs targeting the same small craters at the south pole resolve their disputes without a framework for doing so.

The Moon is governed — nominally — by the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, which forbids sovereignty claims and requires peaceful use. What it does not govern is a $150 billion Artemis program, a competing Chinese-Russian International Lunar Research Station, commercial mining companies planning to extract water ice for rocket fuel, and the Dennis Hopes of the world who have sold five million acres of lunar real estate with zero legal validity. The Moon is the largest body of unresolved property law in history. It is also the staging ground for everything humanity does in space for the next century. Those two facts are not unrelated.

Geography
Regions, features, and the geography of a world with 9,000 named places

The Moon's geography is divided into two fundamental terrain types: the heavily cratered ancient highlands (terrae) that cover most of the far side and the southern near side, and the vast dark volcanic plains (maria) that fill the large impact basins on the near side. This asymmetry — more maria on the near side, more highlands on the far side — remains only partially explained but relates to the thinner crust on the near side during the period of heavy bombardment.

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) governs the naming of all 9,000+ named features on the Moon. Craters, mountains, valleys, rilles, and plains all have formal approved names. The naming tradition is rich: craters are named after scientists, astronomers, artists, and explorers. The maria (seas) — despite containing no water — carry evocative Latin names assigned by 17th-century observers. The most contested real estate in the Solar System is named Shackleton, after the Antarctic explorer, because no one in 1994 when it was named imagined it would become the target of billion-dollar missions from competing superpowers.

The South Pole in detail — Shackleton and the contested craters

The lunar south pole sits on the rim of Shackleton Crater — a 21 km wide, 4.2 km deep impact basin whose rotational axis passes directly through it. The crater floor has been in permanent shadow for approximately 2 billion years, maintaining temperatures of around −183°C. At those temperatures, water ice delivered by ancient comet impacts survives essentially forever.

Surrounding Shackleton is a cluster of named craters — de Gerlache, Sverdrup, Haworth, Nobile, Faustini, Shoemaker, Cabeus — each with their own permanently shadowed interiors. The Shackleton-de Gerlache ridge between the two craters has points that receive near-continuous sunlight — a rare combination in the polar region, making it potentially ideal for a base with both solar power and easy access to ice-bearing craters below.

India's Chandrayaan-3 confirmed the presence of sulfur, iron oxide, aluminum, calcium, chromium, titanium, manganese, silicon, and oxygen near the south pole in 2023 — the first in-situ chemical analysis of the south polar surface. This mission, combined with NASA's LCROSS water ice confirmation (2009), has transformed the south pole from a scientific curiosity into the most strategically valuable real estate off-Earth.

NASA's Artemis program is targeting the Shackleton rim for Artemis Base Camp. China's ILRS is targeting the south pole region. India's Chandrayaan-4 is in development. There is no framework for managing multiple permanent bases in the same small geography.

Naming authority

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) Planetary Nomenclature database governs all officially named lunar features. As of 2025: 9,000+ approved names. Craters are primarily named after deceased scientists, artists, and explorers. No living person may have a crater named after them. Mountains are named after terrestrial mountain ranges or after explorers and scientists.

Notable naming decisions: Shackleton (1994) — named after the Antarctic explorer, predating any awareness it would be the contested south pole crater it is now. Malapert Mountain, near the south pole, named after 17th-century Belgian mathematician Charles Malapert — a key Artemis navigation landmark.

Space Law
The legal framework governing the Moon — five treaties, one fatal gap

Five treaties constitute international space law. All were negotiated between 1967 and 1979, when only the US and USSR had meaningful space capability. They were not written for commercial operators, permanent settlements, or resource extraction. The resulting gaps are not loopholes — they are architectural absences that the 21st century is now stress-testing in real time with billion-dollar programs.

The central gap: the Outer Space Treaty forbids sovereignty over the Moon. But what about using and extracting resources from the Moon? Article II says no "national appropriation" — but does mining water ice constitute appropriation? US law (2015) says no. China and Russia disagree. No international court has ruled. No extraction has occurred. The legal question will be resolved by whoever establishes physical presence first and forces the issue before a framework is agreed.

Outer Space Treaty (OST)
Treaty on Principles Governing Activities in Outer Space
 1967 118 parties Art. II: No sovereignty over Moon or any celestial body "by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means." Art. I: Space is "province of all mankind." Art. IV: No WMDs in space, celestial bodies for peaceful use only. Art. VI: States responsible for ALL national activities including private operators. Art. II bans sovereignty but says nothing about resource ownership. This is the entire legal battleground. Can you own what you mine if you can't own where you mined it? The US says yes. China and Russia are skeptical. No court has decided.
Rescue Agreement
 1968 100 parties States must assist and safely return any astronaut who lands in their territory. Space objects must be returned to the launching state. Emergency assistance is mandatory. Written when "astronaut" meant a state employee. Does not address commercial crew, private astronauts, or tourists. Does not cover what happens if a private company's lander strands occupants.
Liability Convention
 1972 98 parties States absolutely liable for damage caused on Earth by their space objects. In space: fault-based liability. Only successful claim in history: Canada vs. USSR for Cosmos 954 crash (1978), settled for $3M. Does not cover damage between private operators. Commercial debris liability entirely unaddressed. Who is liable if a private mining rover damages another company's equipment?
Registration Convention
 1976 73 parties States must register space objects with the UN. Registering state "retains jurisdiction and control" over that object. Provides basis for flag-of-convenience in space. Compliance inconsistent. Many objects unregistered. A lunar base can fly the flag of any ratifying nation regardless of who operates it.
Moon Agreement (Moon Treaty)
 1979 18 parties Declares Moon and its resources "common heritage of mankind." Any exploitation requires an international regime ensuring equitable benefit-sharing. Prohibits military use. Requires sharing of scientific information. Functionally dead. The US, Russia, and China — the only nations capable of lunar resource extraction — have all refused to ratify it. "Common heritage" implies international oversight of resources. Spacefaring nations want none of that. Only 18 small states have ratified it and none of them can reach the Moon.
Artemis Accords
 2020 61 signatories Non-binding political commitments. Peaceful purposes, transparency, interoperability, emergency assistance, orbital debris mitigation, heritage site protection, safety zones, resource extraction. Covers Moon, Mars, comets, asteroids. 61st signatory: Oman, Jan 26, 2026. Not a treaty — no legal force. Russia and China absent. Russia calls it "a blatant attempt to create international space law that favors the United States." China compares it to colonial enclosure. The nations it needs to matter most haven't signed it.
US Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act
 2015 US domestic law US citizens may own resources they extract from space, even if they cannot own the celestial body itself. Not a sovereignty claim — just ownership of extracted materials. Luxembourg (2017), UAE (2020), Japan (2021) passed similar laws. Does OST Art. VI allow states to authorize private resource extraction that Art. II appears to prohibit? No international court has ruled. The law's authors say yes. Critics say you can't do indirectly what you're prohibited from doing directly.
Ownership & Claims
Who "owns" the Moon — and what that actually means

The short answer: no one. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 — signed by every major spacefaring nation — explicitly forbids national appropriation of the Moon or any celestial body by sovereignty claim, use, or occupation. No nation can own the Moon.

Whether private individuals or corporations can is a genuine legal question. The OST's Article II addresses "national appropriation" — not private appropriation. Entrepreneurs like Dennis Hope have exploited this textual gap since 1980, filing ownership declarations and selling lunar plots. These have no legal validity: no government recognizes them, no court enforces them, and OST Article VI makes states responsible for ALL national space activities including private operators, which effectively means states cannot authorize what they themselves are prohibited from doing.

The more serious question is resource ownership. US law (2015) and similar laws in Luxembourg, UAE, and Japan assert that extracted space resources can be privately owned — even if the body they came from cannot be. The Artemis Accords (non-binding, 61 nations) endorse this position. Russia and China reject it. No extraction has occurred. When it does, the legal question will be forced into the open without a multilateral framework to resolve it.

The Dennis Hope story
5 million acres, zero legal validity, and a surprisingly serious underlying argument

In 1980, a Nevada man named Dennis Hope read the Outer Space Treaty and noticed that Article II forbids "national appropriation" — but says nothing about private individuals. He sent a letter to the United Nations declaring his ownership of the Moon, received no formal objection, and began selling lunar real estate through his Lunar Embassy Commission at approximately $25 per acre.

As of 2025, he claims to have sold over 5 million 1-acre plots to more than 6 million buyers in 193 countries. His claimed customers include Jimmy Carter, Ronald Reagan, and George H.W. Bush (through aides). Several NASA astronauts reportedly have plots. He has allocated land on Mars, Venus, and Io as well.

All of these deeds are legally worthless. No government recognizes them. No court would enforce them. You cannot prevent anyone from landing on "your" plot. You have no mineral rights. You have purchased a novelty certificate. Buying from the Lunar Embassy is not illegal — you simply receive paper, not property.

The underlying argument — that individuals might have property rights the OST doesn't explicitly forbid — has been taken seriously by academic space law journals. The Journal of Air Law and Commerce published a peer-reviewed article (2008) arguing that a true private settlement on the Moon could claim the real estate it needs to survive. This is the legal foundation that the US Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act (2015) partially built on. So Hope is legally wrong in practice — but the kernel of his argument is not entirely frivolous in theory.

Three men from Yemen sued NASA

In 1997, three Yemeni men — Adam Ismail, Mustafa Khalil, and Abdullah al-Umari — sued NASA for trespassing on Mars, claiming they "inherited the planet from our ancestors 3,000 years ago" based on the mythologies of the Himyaritic and Sabaean civilizations. The suit was widely reported as absurd and dismissed. It is included here because it illustrates the global appetite for extraterrestrial property claims and the complete absence of any legal framework for addressing them.

Missions · Historical
From the first crash to the first south pole landing
1959
USSR Luna 2 — first spacecraft to reach the Moon. Hard impact near Mare Imbrium. Luna 3 photographs the far side for the first time in history — revealing a world almost entirely covered in highlands with almost no maria.
1966
USSR Luna 9 — first soft landing (Oceanus Procellarum). Proves the surface can support spacecraft weight — refuting fears of deep dust. US Surveyor 1 lands two months later. Both nations now capable of controlled surface operations.
1966–68
US Lunar Orbiter program — 5 spacecraft map 99% of the lunar surface in preparation for Apollo. Soviet Zond program attempts crewed lunar flyby precursor tests. The race to the Moon is at its most intense.
Jul 20, 1969
Apollo 11 — humans land on the Moon for the first time. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin at Tranquility Base. "One small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." Michael Collins orbits above. 12 humans walk on the Moon total across 6 missions through December 1972.
1970–76
USSR Luna 16, 17, 20, 21, 23, 24 — robotic sample returns and rovers (Lunokhod 1 and 2). Luna 16 returns first robotic samples. Lunokhod 2 drives 37 km — a lunar distance record that stood until 2014 (when Opportunity rover broke it on Mars).
1972–2013
41-year gap in soft landings. No humans return. Science missions continue: Clementine (1994) first suggests polar ice. Lunar Prospector (1998) detects hydrogen at poles, crashed into Shoemaker crater to attempt water vapor detection (result: ambiguous).
2009
NASA LCROSS crashes into Cabeus crater south pole — confirms water ice in the permanently shadowed crater. LRO (Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter) begins comprehensive mapping, still operating as of 2026.
2013
China's Chang'e 3 — first soft landing since 1976. Yutu rover deployed. China's first lunar surface mission establishes its capability and intent for the south pole races to come.
2019
China's Chang'e 4 — first landing on the far side (Von Kármán crater). Yutu-2 rover. India's Chandrayaan-2 orbiter succeeds; lander crashes. Israel's Beresheet crashes. Private lunar landing is harder than it looks.
Aug 2023
India's Chandrayaan-3 — first successful south pole landing. Vikram lander, Pragyan rover. Detects sulfur, iron, calcium, aluminum, silicon, and other elements. Russia's Luna-25 attempts the same region two weeks earlier — crashes. India: 1. Russia: 0. The south pole era begins.
Feb–Mar 2025
Firefly's Blue Ghost — successful US commercial lunar landing (near side). ispace's Resilience — crashes 90 seconds from south pole touchdown. Intuitive Machines IM-2 — lands tilted near south pole area. US commercial lunar economy beginning — messy.

Apollo Program
The 12 humans who walked on the Moon — and what they left behind

Between July 1969 and December 1972, six Apollo missions landed on the Moon. Twelve American men walked on its surface. They collected 382 kg of lunar rock and soil, deployed scientific instruments still returning data decades later, planted six American flags, left plaques, mirrors, seismometers, and the personal effects of the astronauts. They also left approximately 96 tonnes of equipment — descent stages, rovers, scientific experiments — that remain on the surface today, legally protected by nothing except the practical difficulty of getting there.

The Apollo program was the most expensive peacetime scientific undertaking in human history (~$257 billion in 2020 dollars). It was driven by Cold War competition rather than scientific merit — but the science it produced transformed our understanding of the Moon, the Solar System, and Earth. The samples returned by Apollo remain the primary source of ground truth for lunar science 50+ years later.

What is still on the Moon
96 tonnes of human equipment — legally protected by nothing

The six Apollo landing sites contain: six descent stages (the bottom half of the Lunar Module), three Lunar Roving Vehicles (Apollos 15, 16, 17), five ALSEP scientific instrument packages (still returning seismic data until 1977 when funding was cut), two golf balls hit by Alan Shepard, Charles Duke's family photograph in a plastic sleeve, retroreflectors still bounced off by lasers from Earth today (providing the most precise measurement of the Moon's distance and drift), 96 American flags (five of them almost certainly bleached white by radiation), and approximately 96 metric tonnes of equipment total.

NASA's Office of Planetary Protection has issued "NASA's Recommendations to Space-Faring Entities: How to Protect and Preserve the Historic and Scientific Value of U.S. Government Lunar Artifacts" — a voluntary guideline suggesting other missions stay 75–225 meters away from landing sites. It is not binding. The Artemis Accords include a heritage protection principle. It is non-binding. There is no legal instrument that prevents any nation or private company from driving a rover over Tranquility Base today.

Apollo 13 — the one that didn't land

Apollo 13 launched April 11, 1970, targeted for Fra Mauro highlands. On April 13, an oxygen tank in the Service Module exploded, crippling the spacecraft. The crew — Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert, Fred Haise — used the Lunar Module as a lifeboat, circled the Moon without landing, and returned to Earth safely on April 17. "Houston, we've had a problem" (the actual transmission; "we have a problem" is the film version). The successful return was one of NASA's greatest feats of improvised engineering. Fra Mauro was successfully reached by Apollo 14 nine months later.

Science
What we know — and what we don't

The Moon is the best-studied extraterrestrial body in history and we are still learning fundamental things about it. The Apollo samples revolutionized planetary science. LRO has produced the most detailed maps of any body other than Earth. Chandrayaan-3 gave us the first chemical readings of the south pole surface. And we still don't fully understand how the Moon formed, why it has a thicker crust on the far side, or the nature of the "transient lunar phenomena" observed by astronomers for centuries.

Formation — the Giant Impact hypothesis

The leading scientific theory for the Moon's origin is the Giant Impact hypothesis: approximately 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body (named Theia) collided with the proto-Earth at a glancing angle. The impact vaporized and ejected enormous amounts of material. Much fell back to Earth; much entered orbit and coalesced into the Moon. This explains the Moon's similar isotopic composition to Earth, its low iron content (the core material sank back to Earth), and its lack of water (high-temperature impact drove off volatiles).

The hypothesis is well-supported but not fully settled. Recent isotopic analysis of Apollo samples shows the Moon and Earth are almost identical in oxygen, titanium, and chromium isotopes — more similar than the simple Giant Impact model predicts. More sophisticated variants of the hypothesis involve a more head-on impact, a faster-spinning proto-Earth, or multiple smaller impacts. The Moon's exact origin remains an active research area.

Water ice — confirmed and strategic

Water ice on the Moon was confirmed by multiple missions. Clementine (1994) detected hints. Lunar Prospector (1998) found elevated hydrogen at both poles. LCROSS (2009) crashed into Cabeus crater and detected water vapor in the plume. LRO's LAMP instrument and M3 spectrometer on Chandrayaan-1 confirmed surface water ice exposure in permanently shadowed regions. SOFIA airborne observatory (2020) detected water molecules in the sunlit Clavius crater region — surprising, as this was outside the expected polar cold traps.

The ice is not a uniform sheet — it is patchy, concentrated in permanently shadowed craters, mixed with regolith, and exists in various forms (surface frost, subsurface deposits, and possibly ancient buried layers). Exactly how much there is, how accessible it is, and how pure it is at mineable depth are questions that can only be answered by surface missions and drilling — which is exactly what the next generation of missions aims to do.

The strategic implications: water ice at the south pole is rocket propellant. Split by electrolysis into hydrogen and oxygen, it can fuel the next generation of spacecraft for missions to Mars, the asteroid belt, and beyond. A lunar propellant depot could reduce the cost of deep space missions by an order of magnitude. This is why $150+ billion in missions are targeting the same few craters.

Moonquakes — the Moon is not geologically dead

Apollo seismometers (operating 1969–1977) detected four types of moonquakes: deep moonquakes (~700 km depth, tidal origin), shallow moonquakes (near the surface, unexpectedly powerful — up to M5.5), meteorite impacts, and thermal moonquakes (surface cracks due to temperature changes at dawn/dusk). The shallow moonquakes are scientifically puzzling and practically important — any permanent lunar base must be designed to withstand them. They can last for up to 10 minutes (Earth quakes typically last 30–60 seconds).

A 2019 study analyzing LRO imagery found thousands of fresh surface cliffs (lobate scarps) indicating the Moon is still contracting as it cools, producing shallow faulting and quakes. The Moon is geologically active — just very slowly. This has implications for long-term habitation near fault zones and for understanding the Moon's thermal history.

Key discoveries
Lunar lava tubes
LRO discovered large pits (skylights) leading to hollow underground tubes formed by ancient lava flows. Some may be hundreds of meters wide and kilometers long. Temperatures inside remain stable (~−20°C) compared to surface extremes. Natural habitat candidates for bases — no construction required, protected from radiation and meteorites.
Chandrayaan-3 chemical detections
2023 — First in-situ analysis of south polar surface. Detected: sulfur (unexpected — implications for geology), iron oxide, aluminum, calcium, chromium, titanium, manganese, silicon, oxygen. Sulfur presence was surprising and suggests volcanic activity reached the south pole or sulfur was deposited by impacts.
MOXIE oxygen production
Perseverance rover's MOXIE experiment on Mars (2021) demonstrated oxygen production from CO₂. Analogous technology — splitting water ice electrolytically — is planned for lunar ISRU (in-situ resource utilization). Artemis missions will test this on the Moon.
Genesis Rock (Apollo 15)
4.5 billion year old anorthosite sample collected by Apollo 15. Essentially the age of the Solar System — evidence of the Moon's original "magma ocean" crust. One of the most scientifically important rock samples ever collected.
Laser ranging retroreflectors
Apollo 11, 14, and 15 left retroreflectors that are still bounced off by lasers from Earth today. These provide the most precise measurement of the Earth-Moon distance (accurate to a few millimeters) and confirm the Moon is moving away from Earth at 3.8 cm/year.
Open science questions

Despite 60+ years of study, these fundamental questions remain open:

Why is the far side crust thicker? The near/far side asymmetry is pronounced and not fully explained. The Giant Impact model predicts symmetric thicknesses. Something else happened.

What are transient lunar phenomena? Hundreds of reports of localized glows, color changes, and obscurations on the lunar surface from qualified observers over centuries. Some are almost certainly explained (impact flashes, atmospheric effects in the observer's telescope). Some are not satisfactorily explained. The lunar atmosphere (extremely thin, but present) may be involved.

How much ice is actually at the poles, at what depth, and at what purity? Surface estimates vary by orders of magnitude. Drilling data is essential and no drill has reached the permanently shadowed regions.

Future
Three possible Moons — 2026 to 2050
Path 1 — Managed Coexistence
Artemis and ILRS both establish permanent bases at the south pole without direct conflict. UN COPUOS negotiates a resource framework both blocs accept. Safety zones are recognized de facto. The Moon develops like Antarctica — competitive science under a shared minimum framework. Permanent human presence sustained by multiple nations, commercially supplied, no territorial claims asserted. Probability: possible, requires sustained US-China diplomatic engagement that does not currently exist.
Path 2 — The Muddle
Both programs establish bases but in different parts of the south pole. No direct conflict but no framework either. Resource extraction begins under domestic laws with no international oversight. Disputes arise over safety zones, ice access, and radio frequency interference. Each bloc builds its own legal norms through practice. International law catches up slowly and incompletely. The Moon becomes legally complex in the same way international waters did — contested, pragmatically managed, never fully governed. Most likely single outcome.
Path 3 — Facts on the Ground
One bloc establishes presence at the best south pole ice sites before the other. De facto control established without de jure sovereignty claim. The second-arriving bloc finds the prime locations occupied. Disputes escalate without a resolution mechanism. Earth's geopolitical competition replicated on another world. Law follows power — whoever occupies the craters first effectively controls them regardless of what the OST says. The historical precedent of terrestrial resource races offers little comfort.
Permanent habitation
What living on the Moon actually looks like

A permanent lunar base must solve radiation (no magnetosphere, thin surface shielding needed — underground or thick regolith coverage), temperature extremes (127°C to −173°C in 14-Earth-day cycles at equatorial latitudes, more stable at the poles), vacuum (pressurized structures required for everything), dust (lunar regolith is sharp, abrasive, electrostatically charged, and coats everything — killed the seals on Apollo gear), and supply chains (every consumable except water ice and oxygen must come from Earth until true self-sufficiency is established).

The most credible near-term habitat solutions involve inflatable structures covered with regolith for radiation shielding, or utilization of existing lava tubes (naturally shielded, temperature-stable, no construction required). NASA and ESA have run 3D printing in regolith experiments and sintering studies. The basic engineering is understood. The challenge is cost and supply chain — maintaining a crew continuously on the Moon requires a launch cadence and logistics system that doesn't yet exist.

Open variables · 2026
Will Artemis IV land before China?
Artemis IV crewed landing ~2028. China crewed landing ~2030. Race is closer than it looks. Both depend on unproven heavy-lift vehicles (Starship HLS for NASA, Long March 10 for China).
Can Starship HLS deliver?
NASA's crewed landing depends entirely on SpaceX Starship as the Human Landing System. Starship is still in testing. SpaceX delayed Mars missions to focus on lunar. The critical path runs through Boca Chica.
Will COPUOS produce a resource framework?
No treaty has emerged from COPUOS since the Moon Agreement in 1979. The political will for a binding framework requires US-China agreement. Current geopolitical temperature makes this unlikely before physical presence is established.
Will VIPER find mineable ice?
NASA's VIPER rover (delayed from 2023) will prospect south pole ice deposits. The quantity, purity, and accessibility of ice at drillable depths are still unknown. Everything about the south pole's economic value rests on what VIPER and Chang'e 7 find.
Who governs a permanent lunar base?
The ISS Intergovernmental Agreement (1998) is the only model for multi-national space governance. It was negotiated over years among committed partners. No equivalent framework exists for the Moon. Any permanent base will initially be governed by a patchwork of the launching state's domestic law + OST + bilateral agreements.
Why it matters for Earth

The Moon's south pole ice, if accessible, would transform the economics of space. A propellant depot at the lunar south pole — fueled by in-situ water ice electrolysis — would reduce the cost of reaching Mars, the asteroid belt, and beyond by an order of magnitude. Earth-launched missions carry all their fuel from the bottom of Earth's gravity well. Moon-based missions start from a shallower well with local propellant.

The geopolitical stakes are correspondingly high. The nation or coalition that controls lunar propellant controls the logistics of the entire Solar System economy. The Moon is not just a destination — it is a logistics hub. The legal and governance framework (or lack of one) established in the next decade will determine whether that hub is a shared commons or a contested asset.

How to Get There
The physics, engineering, and economics of reaching the Moon

The Moon is 384,400 km away on average — about 30 Earth diameters. Light takes 1.3 seconds to reach it. A typical lunar transit takes 3 days by spacecraft. Apollo took 3 days outbound; Artemis II will follow a similar free-return trajectory taking 3 days to reach lunar vicinity.

Getting to the Moon requires first reaching Earth orbit (needs ~9.4 km/s delta-v from the surface), then performing a Trans-Lunar Injection (TLI) burn to leave Earth orbit (~3.2 km/s more), then a Lunar Orbit Insertion (LOI) to enter lunar orbit (~1.0 km/s), then a Powered Descent Initiation (PDI) and landing (~2.0 km/s). Each step requires enormous energy expenditure. The rocket equation means each kilogram of payload delivered to the lunar surface requires many kilograms of propellant.

The Saturn V — the only rocket ever to send humans to the Moon — produced 35 million newtons of thrust, weighed 2.8 million kg at launch, and delivered ~45 kg of returned samples per mission. NASA's current Space Launch System produces 39 million newtons. SpaceX's Starship is designed to be fully reusable and could dramatically reduce per-kg costs.

Launch windows and orbital mechanics

Lunar launch windows depend on several factors: the Moon's position in its orbit, the desired landing site latitude, and the desired lighting conditions at the landing site (too little sunlight = can't see, too much = high temperatures). Apollo missions targeted early lunar morning at the landing site — long shadows revealed terrain, but temperatures were not yet extreme.

Artemis missions also must consider the Lunar Gateway orbit when it exists. The Gateway's Near Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO) around the Moon is stable and accessible from multiple lunar surface locations — a key advantage over traditional low lunar orbit for a staging station.

Earth-Moon transit time trades off with fuel: Apollo used a direct free-return trajectory (3 days). Some robotic missions use slower, more fuel-efficient low-energy transfer orbits via the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point (3–4 months). Artemis II will use a free-return trajectory — if the main engine fails after TLI, the spacecraft automatically returns to Earth without any burns.

Commercial lunar travel — what is currently possible

As of 2026, no commercial provider offers public crewed lunar travel. The only organization providing crewed access is NASA through Artemis. However:

Axiom Space has flown commercial missions to the ISS at ~$55M per seat. It has discussed future lunar tourism in the Artemis era but no lunar mission is planned. SpaceX sold a circumlunar mission to Japanese entrepreneur Yusaku Maezawa (the "dearMoon" project) using Starship — this was officially cancelled in 2024 after crew members withdrew. Blue Origin has not announced lunar tourist offerings.

The practical reality: lunar access in the 2020s-30s will be dominated by NASA, CNSA, and government-funded science programs. Commercial lunar tourism — if it comes — will likely not be available at any price until the 2040s at the earliest, requiring stable propellant infrastructure and a functioning lunar economy.

Used for commercial lunar landers (CLPS program) and small probes. Cannot carry crew to the Moon. Reliable, cost-effective for robotic lunar missions. Most lunar commercial deliveries in the near term use these.
The lunar environment you are traveling to
Radiation
No magnetosphere. No atmosphere. Surface exposed to solar energetic particles and galactic cosmic rays. A 2-week stay at the lunar surface delivers ~30 mSv — equivalent to ~1 chest CT scan. Long-term habitation requires shielding (regolith, lava tubes, or purpose-built shielding).
Dust
Apollo astronauts' biggest practical complaint. Lunar regolith is extremely fine, jagged (no weathering rounds the particles), and electrostatically charged. It stuck to everything, scratched visors, degraded seals, and was nearly impossible to remove. Every long-duration surface operation must solve the dust problem.
Temperature extremes
Equatorial daytime: +127°C. Equatorial night: −173°C. Poles (sunlit rim): near constant −50°C (much more manageable). Polar permanently shadowed regions: −183°C to −233°C. South pole bases have enormous thermal advantages over equatorial locations.
Communications
1.28 second one-way signal delay. Real-time communication with Earth is fully functional — unlike Mars. Near-side locations have direct line-of-sight to Earth continuously. Far-side requires relay satellites. Artemis Gateway will provide improved relay coverage.

The moon goddesses
She has had a thousand names

In nearly every culture on Earth, the Moon was feminine before it was scientific. The connection is not arbitrary — the Moon's 29.5-day cycle mirrors the average human menstrual cycle with uncanny precision, and the two were observed and named long before anyone knew why. Across hundreds of distinct mythologies, the Moon became the domain of goddesses of fertility, birth, death, healing, magic, time, and the cycles of all living things.

A small handful of cultures assigned the Moon to male deities — most notably Japan (Tsukuyomi), Egypt (Khonsu and Thoth), Norse tradition (Máni), and Mesopotamia (Sin/Nanna). But the overwhelming cross-cultural tendency was feminine. The Moon was the Great Mother, the measurer of time, the protector of women and childbirth, the weaver of fate. She watched over the night while the Sun ruled the day — and the night was always more mysterious, more intimate, more tied to the cycles of the body.

Greek
Selene · Artemis · Hecate
The Greek lunar triple — Selene was the Moon itself, driving a silver chariot across the night sky, daughter of Hyperion and Theia. Artemis governed the crescent and the hunt. Hecate ruled the dark moon, magic, and crossroads. Together they were the Triple Goddess of the lunar cycle's three faces. Selene fell in love with the mortal Endymion and begged Zeus to grant him eternal sleep so his beauty would never fade — she visited him nightly on Mount Latmos, his eyes forever closed.
Roman
Luna · Diana
Luna was the direct personification of the Moon, her influence so pervasive that "luna" gave English its word for lunatic — the belief that the full moon drove people mad. Diana was the huntress, protector of animals and young girls, goddess of childbirth. She was worshipped at Lake Nemi in a sacred grove near Rome in rites so ancient and strange that James Frazer opened The Golden Bough with them. The month name "Monday" descends from "Moon's day" — Lunae dies in Latin.
Incan
Mama Quilla — Mother Moon
Mama Quilla (Kichwa: "Mother Moon") was among the most important deities in the Inca pantheon — sister and wife of Inti the Sun God, protector of married women and the menstrual cycle, regulator of the agricultural calendar. Silver was her sacred metal — the "tears of the Moon." Her primary temple in Cusco was lined with silver to reflect moonlight. During lunar eclipses, when the Moon appeared to be devoured by a heavenly jaguar, the Inca would shout, throw spears, and sacrifice animals to protect her.
Chinese
Chang'e — Lady of the Moon Palace
Chang'e (嫦娥) drank an elixir of immortality intended for her husband, the archer Hou Yi, and floated up to the Moon where she lives in the Cold Jade Palace with a jade rabbit who endlessly pounds the elixir of life with a mortar and pestle. She is a figure of loneliness, longing, and eternal beauty. The Mid-Autumn Festival — held on the full moon of the 8th lunar month — is celebrated in her honor with mooncakes. NASA named the Chinese lunar program after her.
Mayan
Ix Chel — Lady Rainbow
Ix Chel (Ix = Lady; Chel = Rainbow) appeared in two forms — a young, sensual woman associated with fertility and the weaving of time, and a powerful old woman with claws and a serpent headdress associated with floods and destruction. She was goddess of medicine, midwifery, weaving, and the moon. She was often depicted with a rabbit, the classic symbol of lunar fertility. Her sacred island was Cozumel, "Island of the Swallows," the most important pilgrimage site in the Mayan world.
Egyptian
Khonsu · Thoth · Isis
Egypt was unusual in assigning its primary lunar roles to male gods. Khonsu ("He who traverses the sky") was the son of Amun and Mut, depicted as a mummified youth with a crescent moon — healer and measurer of time. Thoth, the ibis-headed god of wisdom and writing, won five extra days from Khonsu in a dice game — days he gave to the sky goddess Nut so she could give birth to the gods. Isis was sometimes identified as a moon goddess, and the crescent moon became her crown.
Celtic
Arianrhod · Cerridwen · Brigid
Arianrhod ("Silver Wheel") was the Welsh goddess of the Moon, stars, and the turning of fate — her wheel was the Moon herself, turning the cycles of birth, death, and rebirth. Cerridwen, the crone of transformation, brewed the cauldron of knowledge in the dark of the moon. Brigid bridged the triple aspect — goddess of poetry, healing, and the forge — associated with the waxing moon's creative power. The Celtic calendar was lunar at its core, with months named for trees.
Hindu
Chandra · Soma
Chandra (चन्द्र, "shining") is the Hindu moon god — young, fair, riding a chariot drawn by antelopes, wearing a crescent on his brow. He married 27 daughters of Daksha — one for each nakshatra (lunar mansion) in the Hindu system. He favored Rohini above the others, and the jealous remaining wives complained to their father, who cursed Chandra to wane. The Moon's monthly waning and waxing is explained by this curse, relieved each fortnight by the god's return. Soma — the sacred ritual drink of the Vedas — and the moon god are often identified as the same.
Astrology
The Moon in Western and Vedic traditions

In Western astrology, the Moon governs the inner emotional self — instinct, memory, habit, the subconscious. Where the Sun is who you are, the Moon is how you feel. It rules Cancer, the cardinal water sign, and is exalted in Taurus. It moves faster than any other celestial body in the natal chart, changing signs approximately every 2.5 days. The Moon's placement at birth — its sign, house, and aspects — describes a person's emotional landscape, their relationship to the past and to their mother, their instinctive responses before the rational mind engages.

In Vedic (Jyotish) astrology, the Moon holds even greater weight than in Western tradition — many Vedic astrologers consider the Moon sign more important than the Sun sign, because it governs mind (manas) and perception. The 27 nakshatras (lunar mansions) divide the zodiac into 27 sections of 13°20' each, corresponding to the Moon's nightly position over 27 days. Each nakshatra has its own deity, symbol, and archetypal quality. The Moon in Jyotish is the lens through which all of life is experienced.

In Chinese astrology, the Moon governs the Yin principle — receptive, interior, feminine, cyclical. In Taoist cosmology, the Moon is pure Yin energy in its most visible form, in constant dialogue with the Yang energy of the Sun. The balance of these two forces — and the Moon's phases as a living record of their relationship — is central to Chinese medicine, divination, and agricultural practice.

The feminine Moon
What the science actually shows
29.5 days — the coincidence that isn't
The average human menstrual cycle is 28–29 days. The lunar synodic cycle is 29.53 days. This near-identity — noticed by every ancient culture — was almost certainly the original motivation for the Moon's deep association with femininity, fertility, and reproductive timing. Whether the resemblance is causal or coincidental is still debated by biologists.
The synchronization studies
A 2021 peer-reviewed study in Science Advances (Helfrich-Förster et al., Würzburg) analyzed long-term menstrual records from 22 women spanning up to 37 years. Finding: women's cycles intermittently synchronized with the Moon's luminance and gravitational cycles — but not permanently, and the effect decreased with age and with exposure to artificial light. Women who were "night owls" showed no synchronization at all. The pre-2010 records showed stronger synchrony than post-2010, suggesting modern artificial lighting has disrupted an ancient biological clock.
Birth rates and the full moon
Two large longitudinal studies — encompassing ~250,000 and ~500,000 births respectively — found birth rates elevated by 2–3% above average at the full moon and reduced by the same amount at the new moon. The effect is small but consistent. The scientific community remains divided on whether it is causal or statistical noise.
Sleep and the full moon
A separate 2021 Science Advances study found that people — urban, rural, indigenous, and non-indigenous alike — fell asleep later and slept less in the days leading up to the full moon. This effect was observed even in urban settings where artificial light outshines moonlight, suggesting a biological clock tuned to the lunar cycle, not just a direct light response.
Fertility and lunar timing
Multiple studies have found that women with menstrual cycles closest to 29.5 days — the most "lunar" cycles — have the highest likelihood of becoming pregnant. Whether this reflects an adaptive synchronization to moonlight for maximizing fertility windows, or simply that 29.5-day cycles reflect optimal ovulatory timing for independent biological reasons, is unresolved.
The word "menstruation"
Comes from Latin menses (months) and Greek mene (moon). "Month" itself comes from "moon" — the Old English monath, from mona (moon). In virtually every Indo-European language, the words for Moon, month, and menstruation share the same root. The semantic linkage is 5,000+ years old and pan-cultural.
Monday — the Moon's day
The seven-day week was organized around the seven classical celestial bodies, each governing one day. Monday = Moon's day (Old English Mōnandæg). In French: lundi (from Luna). In Italian: lunedì. In Spanish: lunes. In virtually every Romance language and many Germanic ones, the second day of the week still carries the Moon's name — a remnant of the ancient lunisolar week embedded in the calendar of nearly every modern nation.
