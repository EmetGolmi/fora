What this jurisdiction is
The Solar System at the moment of arrival

The Solar System extends from the Sun's corona to the outer edge of the Oort Cloud — roughly two light-years in radius, or about 126,000 AU. Most of that volume is empty. But what is not empty is extraordinarily varied: rocky inner planets baked by the Sun, a vast asteroid belt, four enormous outer worlds with ring systems and dozens of moons, a second belt of icy objects beyond Neptune, and a vast cloud of cometary nuclei that defines the system's gravitational boundary.

Humanity has been visiting this system in earnest since 1959. As of March 2026, we have spacecraft actively operating at or en route to Mercury, Venus, Earth's Moon, Mars, the asteroid belt, Jupiter, Saturn, and interstellar space. We have landed on the Moon, Mars, an asteroid, and a comet nucleus. We have returned samples from the Moon, a comet, two asteroids, and (pending) Mars.

The Solar System is also the first and only jurisdiction where space law has been operationally tested. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 remains the governing framework — unamended, untested in any court, and increasingly strained by the speed of human return to deep space.

2026 — The year of arrivals
What happens this year changes our map of the Solar System

BepiColombo reaches Mercury (Nov 6). ESA and JAXA's joint orbiter pair ends an 8-year, 9-billion-km journey. Mercury is the least-explored planet in the Solar System — visited only by Mariner 10 (1974–75) and MESSENGER (2011–15). BepiColombo will produce the most comprehensive map of Mercury ever made, study its anomalously large iron core, and test general relativity close to the Sun's gravity well.

Hera arrives at Didymos (Nov). ESA's mission will survey the crater left by NASA's DART impact in 2022 — the first time humanity deliberately changed the orbit of a celestial body. Hera completes the experiment: we now learn exactly how effective kinetic deflection is, and how to calibrate future planetary defense missions.

Hayabusa2 flies by Torifune (Jul). Japan's veteran asteroid probe — which already returned a sample from Ryugu in 2020 — gets humanity's first close-up look at the near-Earth asteroid Torifune. China's Tianwen-2 enters orbit around Kamoʻoalewa in June, humanity's first dedicated near-Earth asteroid orbiter.

Voyager 1 milestone (Nov). The most distant human-made object will become the first to reach one light-day from Earth — ~25.9 billion km. A radio signal to Voyager 1 now takes 24 hours to arrive. The reply takes another 24 hours. We are operating, in real time, in interstellar space.

Where we stand · March 2026
Active civic issues
Space law is fracturing under commercial pressure
The OST prohibits national sovereignty but not resource extraction. The US (2015), Luxembourg (2017), and UAE (2020) have passed domestic laws authorizing their citizens to own extracted space resources. Russia and China have not recognized these laws. The Artemis Accords (43 signatories) create a competing legal framework. No international body can adjudicate disputes.
Planetary protection is in crisis
SpaceX's Starship program plans to carry hundreds of humans to Mars. Current planetary protection protocols were designed for robotic missions. No international agreement governs the biological contamination of other worlds by humans. Once contaminated, there is no going back — the scientific record of Mars is permanent. The window to get this right may already be closing.
Who governs a permanent Lunar/Martian base?
The OST assigns liability to the "launching state" and requires states to supervise their nationals. But signal delay makes Earth governance structurally impossible beyond 3 light-seconds. Any permanent off-Earth habitat will need local governance — but there is no legal framework for creating one.

No approved mission to return. The most remote planet from the Sun, 30 AU away, takes light 4 hours to reach from Earth.
The Sun
The star that makes everything possible, and impossible

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) — 4.603 billion years old, roughly halfway through its hydrogen-burning life. It contains 99.86% of the total mass of the Solar System. Everything else — all eight planets, all their moons, all the asteroids, all the comets, all the spacecraft — is made from the remaining 0.14%.

The Sun's diameter is 1.39 million km — 109 Earths side by side. Its surface temperature is 5,778 K. Its corona (outer atmosphere) reaches temperatures of 1–3 million K — hotter than its surface, and a mystery that has puzzled solar physicists for decades. Parker Solar Probe has been flying through the corona since 2021, and its findings are beginning to explain this paradox through the role of magnetic switchbacks and Alfvén waves.

The Sun's influence extends outward as the solar wind — a stream of charged particles that creates the heliosphere, a vast magnetic bubble that extends ~120 AU in all directions. This bubble shields the inner Solar System from galactic cosmic rays. When the solar wind strips Mars's thin upper atmosphere, or energizes Jupiter's auroras, or disrupts GPS satellites at Earth, that is the Sun's governance of its system — not by law, but by physics.

The Sun will remain on the main sequence for approximately another 5 billion years, then expand into a red giant, engulfing Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth. Mars and the outer planets will survive, transformed. In perhaps 7 billion years, the Sun will shed its outer layers into a planetary nebula and leave behind a white dwarf — a cooling stellar remnant roughly the size of Earth.

Solar activity
Solar Cycle 25 · 2025–2026 solar maximum

The Sun operates on an approximately 11-year cycle of magnetic activity, moving from solar minimum (few sunspots, quiet) to solar maximum (many sunspots, frequent coronal mass ejections) and back. Solar Cycle 25 began in December 2019. Its peak — the solar maximum — is occurring now, in 2025–2026.

Solar maximum brings heightened risk of geomagnetic storms. A Carrington-level event (1859) today would disable global satellite infrastructure, GPS, power grids, and communications networks — estimated cost: $1–2 trillion in the first year, with recovery taking years. The probability of a Carrington-level event in any given decade is approximately 12%.

ESA's Solar Orbiter is now photographing the Sun's poles for the first time in history, having tilted its orbit to 17° above the ecliptic (rising to 24° after the Dec 2026 Venus flyby). NASA's SunRISE mission — six CubeSats studying solar radio bursts — launches mid-2026.

Solar facts
Type: G2V yellow dwarf
The G denotes temperature class, the 2 its position within that class, and V means it is a main-sequence (hydrogen-burning) star. About 10% of all stars in the Milky Way are G-type. Most stars are smaller and dimmer — M-type red dwarfs constitute ~75% of all stars.
Mass: 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg
333,000× Earth's mass. The Sun's gravitational influence extends to the Oort Cloud, ~100,000 AU — roughly 1.6 light-years. Alpha Centauri is 4.37 light-years away.
Luminosity: 3.83 × 10²⁶ watts
Earth intercepts 1 part in 2.2 billion of total solar output. That fraction — the solar constant, ~1,361 W/m² — drives all weather, all photosynthesis, and most of human civilization's energy economy.
Core temperature: 15 million K
Hot enough for hydrogen fusion via the proton-proton chain. The energy generated in the core takes ~100,000 years to reach the surface by radiation and convection — then 8 minutes to reach Earth.
Rotation: 25 days (equator) · 35 days (poles)
The Sun is a plasma, not a solid — different latitudes rotate at different speeds. This differential rotation twists its magnetic field lines over 11 years, storing energy until it erupts in sunspots, flares, and coronal mass ejections.
Remaining life: ~5 billion years
The Sun has converted approximately half its hydrogen core into helium. When hydrogen runs out, it will expand 200× into a red giant, incinerate the inner planets, then collapse into a white dwarf about the size of Earth.

The outer Solar System
Everything beyond Neptune — the vast, cold, largely unvisited frontier

Neptune at 30 AU feels like the edge of the Solar System. It is not. Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt (30–50 AU), home to Pluto, Eris, and hundreds of thousands of icy bodies. Beyond that, the scattered disc extends to ~100 AU. And beyond that: the Oort Cloud, a vast spherical shell of cometary nuclei extending from perhaps 2,000 AU to 100,000 AU — roughly 1.6 light-years from the Sun.

The Solar System's true boundary — the heliopause, where the solar wind gives way to interstellar space — is at approximately 120 AU. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012. Voyager 2 in 2018. No other spacecraft has. No other spacecraft is en route. The two Voyagers are the only humans-made objects to have ever operated in interstellar space.

Pluto — reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006 after the discovery of Eris, which is slightly more massive — was visited for the first time in 2015 by New Horizons. The images revealed a geologically active world with nitrogen ice plains, water ice mountains, and a heart-shaped basin (Tombaugh Regio). Pluto is far more interesting than anyone expected.

Known dwarf planets
Pluto · 39.5 AU — Largest known dwarf planet. Visited by New Horizons 2015. Has 5 moons; Charon is so large the system is a binary. Geologically active. Heart-shaped nitrogen ice plain.
Eris · 96 AU (avg) — Slightly smaller in volume but slightly more massive than Pluto. Its discovery in 2005 triggered Pluto's reclassification. Has one moon, Dysnomia. Never visited.
Haumea · 43.1 AU — Elongated, fast-rotating (4 hrs). Has a ring and two moons. Never visited.
Makemake · 45.8 AU — No confirmed moons. Covered in methane and nitrogen ice. Never visited.
Ceres · 2.77 AU (Asteroid Belt) — Visited by Dawn mission 2015–18. Has bright salt deposits (faculae) from ancient water activity. The only asteroid-belt dwarf planet.
Voyager 1 · In interstellar space
172 AU from Sun · ~25.7 billion km · Signal delay: ~23.5 hours one way · Launched Sep 5, 1977

Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object in existence. It crossed the heliopause — the boundary between the solar wind and interstellar space — in August 2012 at 121 AU. It is now moving through the interstellar medium, transmitting data about the magnetic field, cosmic ray flux, and plasma conditions between the stars.

In November 2026, Voyager 1 will reach a distance of one light-day from Earth (~25.9 billion km). A radio signal will take 24 hours to reach it. A reply will take another 24 hours. This will be the first time in human history that we operate a functional machine that is one full light-day away.

Voyager 1 carries the Golden Record — a 12-inch gold-plated copper disc containing 115 images, sounds, music, and greetings in 55 languages, selected by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. It includes Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode," Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 2, whale songs, Beethoven's 5th Symphony, a photo of a nursing mother, and the sound of rain.

Voyager 2 · Also in interstellar space
143 AU from Sun · Signal delay: ~19.75 hours one way · Launched Aug 20, 1977
Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited all four outer planets — Jupiter (1979), Saturn (1981), Uranus (1986), and Neptune (1989). It crossed the heliopause in November 2018. Still transmitting. Its instruments measure the interstellar medium from a different direction than Voyager 1, providing stereo coverage of humanity's entry into the space between stars. Expected to communicate until approximately 2030–2035, when RTG power falls below operational threshold.
Planet Nine hypothesis
Is there a ninth planet?

Multiple Kuiper Belt Objects have orbital anomalies that can be explained by the gravitational influence of a large undiscovered planet — perhaps 5–10 Earth masses — orbiting the Sun at 400–800 AU with a period of 10,000–20,000 years. The Caltech astronomers who proposed "Planet Nine" in 2016 (Batygin and Brown) maintain the statistical case is strong.

Critics argue the clustering may be observational bias. The Vera Rubin Observatory (first light 2023) is conducting a survey of the outer Solar System that should resolve the question within the next few years. If Planet Nine exists, it is the Solar System's last undiscovered major body.

The governing framework
One treaty, written in 1967, governs the entire Solar System

The Outer Space Treaty (OST) of 1967 is the foundational document of international space law. It has 118 parties, including all major spacefaring nations. Its core provisions: no national appropriation of outer space or celestial bodies by claim of sovereignty; peaceful use only; states are internationally responsible for their nationals' space activities; states must avoid harmful contamination of space and adverse changes to the Earth's environment.

The OST was written during the Space Race, when only the US and USSR had orbital capability, and was designed to prevent the militarization and territorial partition of the Moon. It succeeded. It also succeeded, accidentally, in creating a framework for the entire Solar System — but that framework was never designed for the situation humanity now faces.

The Treaty has never been violated. It has also never been tested in any court, because no international court has jurisdiction over space disputes. There is no Space Court. There is no Space Police. Enforcement depends entirely on political and diplomatic mechanisms — and those mechanisms were designed for a world in which only two states mattered in space.

The Artemis Accords
A parallel legal framework that Russia and China reject

The Artemis Accords (2020) are bilateral agreements between the US and partner nations, built on OST foundations but extending them to address the realities of 21st-century space exploration: transparency, deconfliction of activities, preservation of heritage sites, extraction and use of space resources, and interoperability of rescue systems. As of 2026, 43 nations have signed.

Russia and China have not signed — and have characterized the Accords as a US-led attempt to create a unilateral legal framework outside the existing UN treaties. China is developing its own lunar program (Chang'e 7 planning for 2026 south pole landing) and has indicated it will operate under its own interpretation of OST. The Accords nations and China are now operating simultaneously at the lunar south pole region, with no agreed deconfliction mechanism.

The critical gaps
Five things the OST cannot handle
Gap 1 · Resource extraction has no international framework
The OST prohibits sovereignty but is silent on resource extraction. The US (2015), Luxembourg (2017), and UAE (2020) have passed domestic laws authorizing citizens to own resources they extract. Russia and China have not recognized these laws. When the first commercial lunar mining operation begins, there is no international arbitrator and no agreed framework. First-mover advantage will likely determine outcomes.
Gap 2 · Signal delay makes state supervision physically impossible
OST Art. VI requires states to authorize and "continuously supervise" their nationals' space activities. Mars is currently 19 minutes away at light speed. Real-time supervision is impossible by physics. Any governance of a Mars colony must be local — but no legal framework exists for creating local governance beyond Earth's jurisdiction.
Gap 3 · Planetary protection is voluntary and pre-commercial
COSPAR's planetary protection guidelines govern biological contamination of other worlds. They were designed for robotic missions. SpaceX intends to send humans to Mars — hundreds of them — in the 2030s. Humans cannot be biologically sterilized. Once human biological material reaches Mars, the scientific search for indigenous Martian life becomes permanently contaminated. There is no treaty governing this. There is no enforcement mechanism.
Gap 4 · No framework for legal personhood of off-Earth humans
A person born on Mars to American parents: are they a US citizen? A citizen of no recognized state? What law governs their contracts, their inheritance, their rights? International law has no answer. The first Martian-born human will be a legal anomaly without a framework. Every previous gap in citizenship law was resolved by expanding existing categories. This one has no existing category to expand.
Gap 5 · No agreed deconfliction at the lunar south pole
The lunar south pole's permanently shadowed craters contain water ice — the most valuable resource in the inner Solar System for fuel production. NASA's Artemis program, China's CLEP program, and multiple commercial operators are all targeting the same small region. The Artemis Accords establish "safety zones" around operations but China has not accepted these. There is no agreed process for resolving conflicting claims to the same resource deposit.
The life question
Are we alone in our own Solar System?

The search for life within the Solar System is the most consequential scientific program in human history. If life is found — anywhere, in any form — it changes everything we understand about the likelihood of life in the universe and our own place in it. If life is found to have arisen independently from Earth life, it establishes that life is effectively inevitable wherever conditions permit. If Mars life turns out to be related to Earth life (via meteorite exchange — panspermia), it raises a different set of questions about life's ultimate origin.

The Solar System currently has at least five candidate locations for extraterrestrial life: Mars (subsurface water, potential biosignatures at Cheyava Falls), Europa (subsurface ocean, hydrothermal venting suspected), Enceladus (active geothermal ocean venting into space), Titan (exotic liquid methane chemistry at the surface, possible water ocean below), and possibly Ganymede and Callisto. Venus's clouds — specifically the 48–60 km altitude region where pressure and temperature resemble Earth's surface — are also a candidate, following the phosphine detection controversy of 2020.

Mars
The best current candidate · March 2026

Perseverance's Cheyava Falls sample contains iron-phosphate spots with calcium sulfate halos — the same pattern produced by microbial iron reduction on Earth. A 2025 study concluded that microbial excretion is the most likely explanation, though abiotic chemistry cannot be ruled out without laboratory analysis of the actual sample. The sample is cached; getting it back requires the Mars Sample Return mission.

Separately: January 2026 brought confirmation of ancient beaches in Jezero Crater (Imperial College London) — Mars had stable, long-duration standing water in its Noachian period. And March 2026 data from InSight confirmed liquid water at 11.5–20 km depth beneath the surface. An environment that once had the right conditions. Possible current liquid water. Organic chemistry confirmed. The question is not whether Mars ever had the conditions for life, but whether life took hold — and whether anything survives.

The ocean worlds
More liquid water than Earth — under ice
Europa · Jupiter's moon
A liquid water ocean twice the volume of all Earth's oceans, beneath an ice shell 15–25 km thick. Tidal heating from Jupiter keeps it liquid. Evidence suggests hydrothermal venting at the seafloor. Europa Clipper (arriving 2030) will assess habitability through 49 flybys. JUICE will also observe Europa. Considered the highest-probability location for complex life beyond Earth.
Enceladus · Saturn's moon
Cassini flew through Enceladus's water geysers and detected molecular hydrogen, silica nanoparticles, and complex organic molecules — the signature of hydrothermal chemistry at the seafloor. A small moon, ~500 km diameter, actively venting its ocean into space. The plume material falls back onto Saturn's rings. If you want to sample an ocean world, Enceladus lets you do it without landing — just fly through the plume. No current mission is returning.
Titan · Saturn's largest moon
The only other world with stable surface liquids — methane and ethane lakes at -179°C. A thick nitrogen atmosphere with complex organic chemistry. Dragonfly (NASA), launching 2028, will fly a rotorcraft across Titan's surface and sample its chemistry. The goal: understand prebiotic chemistry in a liquid-methane environment, and look for evidence of exotic biology based on chemistry unlike anything on Earth.
Venus clouds · 48–60 km altitude
Surface: 465°C, 90 atm, sulfuric acid rain. Hostile to any known life. But at 48–60 km altitude, temperature is 30–70°C and pressure is 1 atm — essentially Earth surface conditions. The 2020 phosphine detection (later disputed) pointed at this zone. Rocket Lab's Venus Life Finder targets 2026 launch. A long-shot candidate — but one that, if confirmed, would be reachable by balloon or aircraft habitat.
The Solar System in human history
How our ancestors mapped the sky and named the wanderers

For most of human history, the Solar System did not exist as a concept. What existed were five wandering stars — planets — visible to the naked eye moving against the fixed stars: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. The Sun and Moon were often counted with them, making seven wanderers in total. Seven became sacred across nearly every civilization — seven days of the week, seven notes in the musical scale, seven metals in alchemy, seven archangels in Jewish and Christian tradition.

Babylonian astronomers tracked the wanderers with extraordinary precision from at least 1500 BCE, using them to predict celestial events, agricultural seasons, and the outcomes of wars. Their system was transmitted to the Greeks around 300 BCE, who provided the theoretical framework (a geocentric model with epicycles) that Ptolemy codified in the Almagest (150 CE). This model was good enough to predict planetary positions accurately for 1,400 years.

Copernicus proposed heliocentrism in 1543 — a hypothesis, he called it. Galileo confirmed it in 1610 with a telescope: Jupiter had moons, Venus had phases, the Milky Way was not a cloud but stars. Within a century, Newton had explained why: universal gravitation. Within three centuries, we had spacecraft at every planet in that system.

The speed of this transition — from geocentric cosmology to interplanetary spacecraft in 400 years — is perhaps the most extraordinary cognitive achievement in human history. Every generation from Galileo to the Apollo astronauts was separated by only a few human lifetimes.

The seven wanderers and the week
Why Monday through Sunday map onto the Solar System

The seven-day week is a Babylonian invention, transmitted through Hellenistic culture into Rome and from Rome into almost every subsequent civilization. Each day was assigned to one of the seven classical planets, in the order of their ancient cosmological sequence (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon), cycling through each hour of the day. The planet ruling the first hour of each day gave that day its name.

The result: Sunday (Sun), Monday (Moon — French: Lundi), Tuesday (Mars/Týr — French: Mardi), Wednesday (Mercury/Woden — French: Mercredi), Thursday (Jupiter/Thor — French: Jeudi), Friday (Venus/Frigg — French: Vendredi), Saturday (Saturn). Every week, every person on Earth observes a sequence whose structure was determined by Babylonian cosmology, 2,500 years ago, based on the visible planets of the Solar System. The Solar System names your week. It always has.

Planetary mythology — the ancient names
☿ Mercury / Hermes — Messenger of the gods. Fastest planet (88-day year), hence the fleet-footed messenger. Patron of trade, travelers, thieves, and communication. Named for his speed, observed darting close to the Sun and vanishing.
♀ Venus / Aphrodite — Goddess of love and beauty — the brightest object in the sky after the Sun and Moon. The Babylonians called it Ishtar. So bright it casts shadows. The morning star and evening star were once thought to be two different objects; Pythagoras is credited with recognizing they were one.
♂ Mars / Ares — God of war — the blood-red wanderer. Named consistently for war by every civilization that tracked it: Nergal (Babylon), Ares (Greece), Mars (Rome), Mangala (India), Her Desher "the red one" (Egypt). The color alone was enough. Red meant blood. Blood meant Mars.
♃ Jupiter / Zeus — King of the gods — the largest and most majestic planet. Babylonians called it Marduk, ruler of the gods, who slew the primordial chaos monster Tiamat to create the world. Jupiter's 12-year orbit around the Sun gave rise to the 12-year cycle in Chinese astrology and the 12 signs of the Western zodiac.
♄ Saturn / Kronos — The old god — outermost known planet to antiquity, slowest-moving, associated with time, age, restriction, and death. Kronos devoured his children. Saturday is Saturn's day. In alchemy, Saturn's metal was lead — the heaviest, the basest. Melancholy was Saturn's humor. Farmers and old men were Saturn's people. Its rings, of course, were unknown until Galileo's telescope in 1610 — and even then he misread them as two companion spheres.
⛢ Uranus / Ouranos — The first planet discovered by telescope — William Herschel, 1781. Initially named Georgium Sidus ("George's Star") after King George III by its discoverer. The international astronomy community eventually settled on Uranus, father of the Titans. The first planet whose existence was unknown to antiquity.
♆ Neptune / Poseidon — Discovered by mathematical prediction in 1846 — the first planet found by calculation before observation. Its position was independently predicted by Adams (England) and Le Verrier (France), who calculated where an unseen planet must be to explain Uranus's orbital perturbations. Galle in Berlin pointed his telescope at Le Verrier's coordinates and found Neptune within 1° of the prediction. The most intellectually dramatic discovery in planetary astronomy.

☀ The Sun

G2V yellow dwarf star. Age: 4.603 billion years. 99.86% of the Solar System's total mass. Surface temperature: 5,778 K. Core temperature: 15 million K. Luminosity: 3.83 × 10²⁶ watts. Diameter: 1.39 million km (109 Earths). Distance from Earth: 1 AU = 149.6 million km. Light travel time: 8 minutes 20 seconds.

The Sun will remain on the main sequence for approximately 5 more billion years, then expand into a red giant, then collapse to a white dwarf.

The Eight Planets

Rocky inner planets (terrestrial)

Mercury (0.39 AU), Venus (0.72 AU), Earth (1.0 AU), Mars (1.52 AU) — small, dense, rocky, with little or no atmosphere except Venus (thick CO₂) and Earth (N₂/O₂).

Giant outer planets

Jupiter (5.2 AU, gas giant), Saturn (9.5 AU, gas giant with rings), Uranus (19.2 AU, ice giant), Neptune (30.1 AU, ice giant). The gas giants are mostly hydrogen and helium; the ice giants contain water, ammonia, and methane ices beneath hydrogen envelopes.

Beyond the planets

The Kuiper Belt (30–50 AU), scattered disc, and Oort Cloud (2,000–100,000 AU) contain hundreds of thousands of icy bodies including dwarf planets Pluto, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake.

Voyager 1 · The Golden Record

Voyager 1 is 172 AU from the Sun as of March 2026 — approximately 25.7 billion kilometers. It crossed into interstellar space in August 2012. Signal travel time: ~23.5 hours one way.

In November 2026 it will become the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth (~25.9 billion km). A round-trip communication will then take 48 hours.

The Golden Record

Both Voyagers carry a gold-plated copper disc containing 115 images, natural sounds (rain, surf, birds, whales), spoken greetings in 55 languages, and 90 minutes of music — including Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 2, Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode," Beethoven's 5th Symphony, Azerbaijani folk music, and Peruvian pan pipes. The contents were selected by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. The record is expected to remain intact for at least a billion years.

Outer Space Treaty · 1967

Signed January 27, 1967. Entered into force October 10, 1967. 118 parties as of 2026, including all major spacefaring nations.

Core provisions

Art. I: Outer space is the province of all mankind, free for exploration and use by all states. Art. II: No national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, use, or occupation. Art. IV: No weapons of mass destruction in orbit or on celestial bodies. Military bases and maneuvers on celestial bodies prohibited. Art. VI: States responsible for national activities in outer space, including those of non-governmental entities. Art. IX: States must avoid harmful contamination of space and adverse changes to the Earth's environment.

What it doesn't cover

Resource extraction, citizenship of off-Earth humans, commercial activities, deconfliction of competing operations, enforcement mechanisms, and governance of permanent settlements.

The Scale of the Solar System

If the Sun were the size of a grapefruit (12 cm), Earth would be 13 meters away — the size of a grain of sand. Neptune would be 400 meters away. The nearest star (Alpha Centauri) would be 3,500 km away.

Light travel times

Sun → Earth: 8 min 20 sec · Sun → Mars (avg): ~12.5 min · Sun → Jupiter: 43 min · Sun → Saturn: 1 hr 20 min · Sun → Neptune: 4 hrs 10 min · Sun → Voyager 1: ~23.5 hrs · Sun → nearest star: 4.37 years

The Oort Cloud

The Solar System extends to the Oort Cloud — approximately 100,000 AU or ~1.6 light-years. Voyager 1 will reach its inner edge in approximately 300 years and pass through it in approximately 30,000 years. Alpha Centauri is 4.37 light-years away — not much further than the Oort Cloud's outer boundary.
