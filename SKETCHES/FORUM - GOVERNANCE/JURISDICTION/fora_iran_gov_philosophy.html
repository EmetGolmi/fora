What Iran Believes — The Doctrine That Runs the State
Every state has a governing philosophy — a doctrine that answers the question of where political authority comes from and why it should be obeyed. Iran's answer is Velayat-e Faqih: guardianship of the Islamic jurist. It is not a policy preference. It is the constitutional foundation of the Republic, placed above all elected institutions, and it was specifically designed so that no election could dislodge it. Whether that design is legitimate, wise, or just is a CivicIssue. What it says and how it works is recorded here.

How the Doctrine Encodes Itself in Law —
Who Counts, and How Much
Governing philosophy is not abstract. It materializes in the law — in who can hold office, who inherits property, whose death generates restitution, who can build a house of worship, who must declare a religion on their national identity card. The 5 reserved seats in parliament are the state's own answer to the question of which communities it recognizes as legitimately existing. Everything below that line is the answer to what happens to communities it does not.

Legal hierarchy of persons · encoded in law · not a caste system in name
Iran does not have a formal caste system. What it has is a constitutional hierarchy of legal personhood — rights, protections, and the weight of a life in law that differ by religion. This is not discrimination in practice only; it is discrimination encoded in statute.

Blood money (Diyeh)
Full diyeh for Muslim men. Reduced diyeh for women (half of male Muslim). Recognized religious minorities: equal to Muslim since 2004 amendment. Baha'i: diyeh classified as mubah — licit to spill, no legal restitution.
Diyeh is the financial compensation owed to a victim's family for death or injury. Its differential application by religion encodes the legal value of a life.
 Islamic Penal Code Art. 297 (amended 2004) · Art. 310 · Judicial interpretations re: Baha'i
Presidency
Restricted to male Twelver Shia Muslims who are Iranian-born and have "managed affairs" and "organizational ability." Non-Shia cannot run. All minority religious groups, including Sunni, are constitutionally barred.
 Constitution Art. 115
Judiciary
Judges must be Muslim and male. Non-Muslims cannot become judges. Judges are required to rule according to the Quran and Islamic jurisprudence — structurally excludes all non-Muslims from judicial roles.
 Constitution Art. 163 · Islamic Penal Code
Inheritance
If any heir converts to Islam, they receive the entire inheritance — disinheriting all non-Muslim family members. Creates systematic economic pressure to convert.
 Islamic law as applied by Iranian courts · Verified by Human Rights Watch
Social pension (Baha'i)
Baha'is are explicitly banned from the national social pension system. Not a general non-Muslim exclusion — targeted specifically at Baha'i.
 Verified by US Dept of State Religious Freedom Report · multiple years
Public employment screening
All applicants for public sector employment are screened for "adherence to and knowledge of Islam" regardless of faith. Members of recognized minorities can serve in lower ranks only. Baha'i and unrecognized minorities barred entirely from government employment and military leadership.
 Constitution Art. 117 · Civil Service Recruitment Law
University admission (Baha'i)
Baha'is are systematically denied registration at public universities because of their faith. In 2007 Iran established the "Baha'i Institute for Higher Education" (BIHE) to serve the community — Iranian authorities have raided it multiple times.
 UN Special Rapporteur reports · Human Rights Watch documentation
Marriage recognition
Baha'i marriages are not legally recognized by the state. Baha'i couples must marry under civil law. Baha'i children born of unrecognized marriages face legal complications in inheritance, nationality, and civil status.
 Department of Justice documentation · Freedom of Religion in Iran reports
Historical note · Jizya · جزیه
The Predecessor System — Not Currently Levied
The jizya — a poll tax on non-Muslim "People of the Book" (Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians) in exchange for protection and exemption from military service — was a central feature of Islamic governance for over a millennium. It was paid by Zoroastrians in Iran until 1882, when it was abolished under pressure from the Persian Zoroastrian Amelioration Fund and British diplomatic influence. Jews in Iran paid jizya until approximately 1933. The Islamic Republic does not formally levy a jizya. The modern equivalent, however, is structural rather than monetary: differential legal personhood (diyeh, pension exclusion, inheritance law, university admission bans) achieves a similar hierarchy of civic standing without a named tax. In 2016, Muslim scholars from over 100 countries signed the Marrakesh Declaration, arguing the dhimmi/jizya framework is obsolete in the modern era of citizenship-based states. Iran was not among the signatories.
