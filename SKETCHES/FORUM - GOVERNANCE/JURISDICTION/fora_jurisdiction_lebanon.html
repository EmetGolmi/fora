Overview
Lebanon in 2026 — a country at a hinge point

Lebanon is a country that has spent the last four decades of its existence contested from within and without. A confessional system that assigns government offices by religious sect — the president must be Maronite Christian, the prime minister Sunni Muslim, the parliamentary speaker Shia Muslim — has produced a political culture in which governance is perpetually held hostage to sectarian arithmetic. The Taif Agreement of 1989 ended the civil war but institutionalized the deadlock.

For most of the past two decades, that deadlock was amplified by Hezbollah — a Shia militant and political organization that functioned as a state within the state, providing social services, running a military force more powerful than the Lebanese Army, and blocking presidential candidates it did not approve of. Michel Aoun, whose term ended in October 2022, was the last president Hezbollah could support. After twelve failed parliamentary votes over two years, the 2024 war between Israel and Hezbollah changed the calculation.

Hezbollah lost most of its senior leadership to Israeli airstrikes in 2024, its secretary-general Hassan Nasrallah was killed, and its military infrastructure was severely degraded. In this weakened state it could no longer veto a presidential election. On January 9, 2025, Joseph Aoun — the Lebanese Army commander, backed by Saudi Arabia and the United States — was elected president on the parliament's thirteenth attempt. Nawaf Salam, a former International Court of Justice judge and former UN Ambassador, was named prime minister in February 2025.

The new government has done things no Lebanese government has done in a generation: it voted to require Hezbollah's disarmament, deployed the Lebanese Army across the south for the first time in forty years, and asserted the state's monopoly on the use of force. Whether it can sustain this trajectory — against Hezbollah's resistance, Israel's continued airstrikes, an $11 billion reconstruction bill, and parliamentary elections in May 2026 — is the central question of Lebanon's current moment.

Government
The Aoun-Salam government — a genuine break

Lebanon's new leadership represents a structural departure from the governments that preceded it. President Joseph Aoun is a Maronite general who built close relationships with the United States and Saudi Arabia while commanding the Lebanese Armed Forces. He is not part of the traditional political class — no family political dynasty, no sectarian militia, no entanglement with the corruption networks that have defined Lebanese governance since the civil war.

Prime Minister Nawaf Salam was Lebanon's Ambassador to the United Nations for twelve years and served as a judge on the International Court of Justice. He is also not part of the traditional Sunni political establishment — he is a technocrat and internationalist by background. His government's 24 ministers were selected with an unusual emphasis on competence over sectarian appointment.

The structural constraint both men face is that the confessional system still governs Lebanon's constitutional order. Parliament Speaker Nabih Berri, head of the Amal movement and Hezbollah's closest political ally, has held his position since 1992. He is not going anywhere. The Hezbollah-aligned bloc holds five of 24 cabinet posts and boycotted the August 2025 vote to begin disarmament. The new government is genuinely different — but it is operating within a system designed to prevent exactly the reforms it is pursuing.

Parliament
128 seats · Last elected 2022

Lebanon's parliament has 128 seats allocated equally between Christians (64) and Muslims (64), with seats further divided among 18 recognized sects. The last parliamentary elections were May 2022. Elections scheduled May 2026.

Current balance of key blocs:

Hezbollah + Amal (Shiite bloc): ~27 seats. The bloc holds less influence than before the 2024 war but retains sufficient seats to complicate disarmament legislation.

Change bloc (civil society, post-2019 revolution): ~13 seats. The closest to the Aoun-Salam reformist agenda.

Lebanese Forces (Christian, Geagea): ~19 seats. Anti-Hezbollah. Supported Aoun's election.

Free Patriotic Movement (Christian, Bassil): ~21 seats. Founded by Michel Aoun. Historically close to Hezbollah. Now positioning away from it post-war.

International Backing
The Quintet framework

Lebanon's current government was brought to power through coordinated pressure by the "Quintet" — United States, France, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Egypt. All five had been trying for two years to break the presidential deadlock.

US: Primary funder of the Lebanese Armed Forces. Close relationship with Aoun. Certification of disarmament phases. Military monitoring mechanism.

Saudi Arabia: Sees Lebanon as an opportunity to rebuild influence lost during the Hezbollah years. Financial reconstruction aid linked to reform progress.

World Bank: $250M reconstruction project approved mid-2025. $11B total estimated need.

France: Historic cultural and colonial relationship. Active diplomatic role in ceasefire negotiations.

The Hezbollah Question
What disarmament actually requires

Hezbollah is not simply an armed group that can be disarmed by a government decision. It is a state within a state that provides services to hundreds of thousands of Lebanese Shia citizens — schools, hospitals, construction companies, social welfare. Its military wing is more capable than the Lebanese Armed Forces. Its political wing holds parliamentary seats and cabinet posts. Disarmament is not a security operation. It is a restructuring of Lebanon's entire political economy.

The Lebanese government's approach has been graduated and conditional. The five-phase plan adopted September 5, 2025 begins in the least politically contested territory (south of the Litani) and moves north. Phase 1 was completed — the LAF achieved operational control south of the Litani for the first time in 40 years. 90% of Hezbollah's military outposts in the south were handed over.

The obstacle is not phase 1. It is phases 2 through 5. Hezbollah has agreed to withdraw south of the Litani — it frames this as complying with the ceasefire agreement. It has refused to relinquish its arsenal north of the Litani, citing two conditions: Israeli withdrawal from occupied Lebanese territory, and Israeli cessation of airstrikes. These conditions create a circular logic. Israel says it will not withdraw until Hezbollah is fully disarmed. Hezbollah says it will not fully disarm until Israel withdraws. Neither side is in a hurry to break the cycle.

Hezbollah's secretary-general Naim Qassem has warned that full disarmament "may lead to civil war and internal strife." This is the statement of a weakened organization trying to retain deterrence, not a genuine threat assessment — but in Lebanon's history, that distinction has sometimes been negligible.

Hezbollah's position
How Hezbollah frames its own situation

FORA does not endorse or condemn Hezbollah's framing. Both the framing and the critique of it are recorded here for civic literacy.

What Hezbollah says: It is a resistance organization defending Lebanon against Israeli aggression. Its weapons are a Lebanese strategic asset. Disarmament while Israeli forces occupy Lebanese soil and conduct daily airstrikes would leave Lebanon defenseless. The LAF is not capable of defending Lebanon against Israel. Phase 1 compliance demonstrates good faith. Phase 2 requires Israeli reciprocity.

What critics say: Hezbollah started the October 7 escalation by firing into Israel one day after the Hamas attack — it brought the war to Lebanon without Lebanese government consent. Its weapons have never defended Lebanese civilians against Israeli attack; they have consistently made Lebanon a theater of Iranian proxy warfare. The "resistance" framing serves Iranian interests, not Lebanese ones. The Lebanese state cannot function as a state while Hezbollah maintains a parallel military.

The structural reality: Hezbollah's Shia constituency in the south and Dahiyeh is real. They rely on Hezbollah's social services. They share its fear of Israeli aggression. Any disarmament framework that does not address this community's legitimate security concerns and service needs will fail regardless of international pressure.

Economy
Financial collapse, reconstruction debt, and the reform imperative

Lebanon's economy has been in freefall since 2019, when the government defaulted on its sovereign debt — the first time Lebanon had ever done so. The Lebanese pound lost more than 90% of its value. The banking system froze depositors' accounts. The Beirut port explosion in August 2020 destroyed the country's primary trade infrastructure and killed more than 220 people. The 2023–2024 war added an estimated $11 billion in reconstruction costs on top of an economy that was already in collapse.

GDP in 2024 was approximately $22 billion — roughly half of what it was in 2018. Unemployment is estimated at 30–40%. More than half the population lives below the poverty line. The Lebanese pound trades at approximately 90,000 per US dollar on the official rate and somewhat lower on the parallel market.

The new Salam government has begun banking sector restructuring — the central reform that international creditors have demanded before releasing IMF and World Bank funds. Progress has been made but the structural problems are deep: Lebanon's banking system was built around a Ponzi scheme of high-interest government bonds that collapsed in 2019, and untangling who loses and how much is politically explosive in a country where bank depositors include every major political family.

Reconstruction funding is gated on reform progress. The World Bank's $250 million project approved mid-2025 is a fraction of the $11 billion needed. Saudi Arabia and Gulf states have indicated willingness to contribute but require visible governance reform and demonstrated Hezbollah disarmament progress before committing large sums.

Lebanese Diaspora
14–20M Lebanese abroad

More people of Lebanese descent live outside Lebanon than within it. Major diaspora: Brazil (~7M), United States (~3M), Argentina (~1.5M), Australia, France, West Africa. Remittances are a critical economic lifeline — often exceeding 20% of GDP in crisis years. The diaspora has historically been a source of capital, political pressure, and the occasional foreign intervention. Under the current crisis, emigration of educated young Lebanese has accelerated dramatically.

History
From Phoenician coast to confessional republic

Lebanon's territory has been continuously inhabited for more than 7,000 years. The Phoenicians — who gave the Western world its alphabet — originated on this coast. Their city-states, Tyre and Sidon, were among the most commercially dominant in the ancient Mediterranean. The Cedar of Lebanon was traded across the ancient Near East. The Lebanese are among the primary genetic descendants of the Phoenicians.

The modern state of Lebanon was carved from the Ottoman Empire by France under the League of Nations mandate after World War I. French strategy deliberately created a majority-Christian state — the Greater Lebanon of 1920 — by incorporating more Muslim territory than the Maronite heartland of Mount Lebanon. This demographic engineering planted the seeds of every subsequent conflict.

1920
France creates Greater Lebanon under League of Nations mandate. Maronite Christians and Sunni/Shia Muslims in uneasy coexistence.
1943
Lebanese independence. The National Pact — an unwritten agreement allocating government posts by sect based on 1932 census. President: Maronite. PM: Sunni. Speaker: Shia.
1975–90
Lebanese Civil War. ~120,000 killed. Syria occupies Lebanon from 1976. Israel invades twice (1978, 1982). PLO expelled 1982. Hezbollah founded 1982 during Israeli occupation.
1989
Taif Agreement ends the civil war. Reallocates parliamentary seats 50-50 Christian-Muslim. President loses executive powers to the cabinet. Syria's occupation continues.
2005
Cedar Revolution. PM Rafiq Hariri assassinated (attributed to Syria/Hezbollah). One million march in Beirut. Syria withdraws troops after 29 years.
2006

October 17 revolution. Mass protests against the entire political class across all sects. Government resigns. The economic collapse begins.
2020
August 4: Beirut port explosion. 2,750 tons of improperly stored ammonium nitrate detonates. 218 killed. Half of Beirut damaged. No one held accountable as of 2026.

2023–24
Hezbollah enters the war alongside Hamas after October 7. Israel systematically eliminates Hezbollah's leadership. Ceasefire November 2024.

Joseph Aoun elected president. Nawaf Salam named PM. First functioning government in three years. Hezbollah disarmament phase 1 completed. Phase 2 negotiations ongoing.
External Relations
Lebanon's contested sovereignty

Lebanon's formal diplomatic position is neutrality and sovereignty. Its actual position has been, for most of its modern history, a battleground for external powers. Syria occupied it for 29 years. Iran built and funds its most powerful political-military organization. Israel has invaded it twice and conducted thousands of airstrikes. Saudi Arabia and the United States back its current government and hold its reconstruction funding.

The Aoun-Salam government is the most genuinely sovereignty-oriented Lebanese government in decades. President Aoun told Iran's representative explicitly that Lebanon "rejects any foreign intervention in its domestic affairs." He condemned Hezbollah's March 2, 2026 attacks. These are not empty gestures — they are direct confrontations with relationships that have governed Lebanese politics for forty years.

Israel-Lebanon
Formal enemies, fragile ceasefire

Lebanon and Israel are formally at war. No peace treaty has ever been signed. Israel is classified as an "enemy state" under Lebanese law. The November 2024 ceasefire is a military arrangement, not a political settlement.

Since the ceasefire, Israel has conducted more than 2,000 violations — airstrikes targeting what Israel calls Hezbollah rearmament activity. The Lebanese government has protested each violation to the US-led monitoring mechanism. Israeli forces continue to occupy five border sites in southern Lebanon. Netanyahu has said Hezbollah's disarmament progress is "far from sufficient."

Direct civilian-level Israel-Lebanon dialogue has begun for the first time — meetings in Naqoura in December 2025 focused on reconstruction and economic cooperation. This is unprecedented. A formal peace process remains distant but is no longer structurally impossible.

Trajectory
Three possible Lebanons

Lebanon's trajectory from here is genuinely uncertain. Three plausible futures present themselves — each dependent on variables that are, as of March 2026, unresolved.

Path 1 — The Chehab Moment
Aoun proves to be a Fouad Chehab — the army commander who became president in 1958 and built Lebanese state institutions from scratch. Disarmament advances through phases 2 and 3. May 2026 elections shift the parliamentary balance against Hezbollah. IMF program unlocks reconstruction funding. A generation of Lebanese who left during the crisis begins to return. The confessional system begins to erode not through constitutional change but through irrelevance. Probability: possible, requires sustained international support and Hezbollah restraint.
Path 2 — The Muddle
Phase 1 is the high-water mark of disarmament. Hezbollah digs in north of the Litani. The circular logic — no disarmament without Israeli withdrawal, no Israeli withdrawal without disarmament — hardens into a permanent condition. Reconstruction funding trickles in but not at the scale needed. Emigration continues. The Aoun-Salam government survives but does not transform the country. Lebanon stabilizes at a lower equilibrium. Probability: the most likely single outcome.
Path 3 — Renewed Escalation
The March 2, 2026 Hezbollah attack on Israel — condemned by Aoun — is a preview. If Iran's regional situation deteriorates further (the Iran war page is still active), Hezbollah may be drawn back into direct conflict before disarmament is complete. Israel's "prevention" timeline and Lebanon's "gradual" timeline do not align. A miscalculation triggers another round. The Aoun-Salam government survives politically but Lebanon's reconstruction window closes. Probability: elevated while Iran's war status remains active.
