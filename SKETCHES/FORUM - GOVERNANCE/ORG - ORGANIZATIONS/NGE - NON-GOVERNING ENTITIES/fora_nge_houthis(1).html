Overview
What the Houthis are — and what makes them hard to categorize

The Houthis — formally Ansar Allah, "Partisans of God" — are simultaneously five things: a Zaydi Shia religious revival movement; a militant organization; the de facto governing authority of northern Yemen including Sanaa; a member of Iran's Axis of Resistance; and, since October 2023, a global maritime security threat with the demonstrated capacity to disrupt one of the world's critical shipping lanes.

Understanding all five simultaneously is necessary to understand the Houthis at all. Organizations that focus only on the maritime threat misunderstand why military responses have had "limited success" — as the New York Times put it in April 2025. The Houthis operate a dispersed, resilient system: mobile weapons, tens of thousands of fighters, and high tolerance for damage. They absorbed the destruction of Sanaa's airport, the elimination of Hodeidah port, the killing of their entire governing cabinet in a single airstrike, and the killing of their military chief of staff — and continued operating.

They are not, despite Western characterization, simply an Iranian proxy. Iran provides weapons, training, and ideological alignment — but the Houthis have their own political base, their own internal logic, and their own interests which do not always align with Tehran's. The Gaza ceasefire triggered their suspension of Red Sea attacks without any Iranian instruction. Their February 28, 2026 threat to resume attacks in response to the Iran war was a choice — not an order.

The Houthi movement began in the 1990s as a religious education network founded by Hussein Badreddin al-Houthi in Saada, northwestern Yemen. It grew into an insurgency against the Yemeni government, fought six wars with Sanaa between 2004 and 2010, and seized the capital in September 2014. The Saudi-led military coalition intervened in March 2015 to restore the internationally recognized government. Ten years later, the Houthis still control Sanaa.

What they call themselves

Ansar Allah — "Partisans of God." Their official name. They reject the "Houthi" label (which comes from the family name of their founder) as a foreign imposed designation.

The Resistance Movement — They frame themselves as part of the broader Axis of Resistance against Israeli and American imperialism. The Gaza campaign was their most successful international branding exercise — it positioned them as the only party actually fighting Israel while Arab states normalized.

The Government of Change and Reconstruction — Their formal governing apparatus in Sanaa. Largely destroyed in the August 2025 Israeli airstrike but reconstituted.

Red Sea Campaign
How Yemen reshaped global trade — twice

On October 19, 2023 — twelve days after the Hamas attacks in Gaza — the Houthis launched their first missile at Israel and their first attack on a commercial vessel in the Red Sea. What followed was the most significant disruption to global maritime trade since the Suez Crisis of 1956.

The Bab el-Mandeb Strait — the narrow chokepoint between Yemen and Djibouti at the southern entrance to the Red Sea — carries approximately 15% of global trade and 12% of global oil shipments. When the Houthis demonstrated they could hit vessels in this strait and in the Red Sea beyond, shipping companies faced a choice: accept the risk, or divert around the Cape of Good Hope. Most chose diversion. This added 11,000 nautical miles, ten days, and approximately $1 million in fuel costs to each voyage. Container shipping through the Red Sea fell 90% between December 2023 and February 2024.

The United States organized Operation Prosperity Guardian — a multinational naval coalition — to defend shipping. US and UK forces conducted hundreds of strikes against Houthi launch sites. The Houthis absorbed the strikes and continued attacking. In April 2025, the New York Times reported that US strikes were "burning through munitions with limited success."

The campaign paused in stages. A US-Houthi ceasefire brokered by Oman in May 2025 removed US vessels from the target list in exchange for halting US strikes on the Houthis. The Houthis continued attacking non-US vessels until the Gaza ceasefire in October 2025, when they suspended all attacks. There have been no Red Sea incidents since September 2025. But the threat has not been removed — it has been placed in abeyance. Suez Canal transit was still 65% below June 2023 levels as of March 2026. Shipping companies have not returned.

Why military responses had limited effect

The failure of Operation Prosperity Guardian to end Houthi attacks — despite hundreds of strikes — illustrated a fundamental mismatch between counterinsurgency logic and deterrence logic. Deterrence requires the threatened party to value what you threaten to destroy. The Houthis did not.

They had already survived a decade of Saudi-led coalition airstrikes that had destroyed most of Yemen's civilian infrastructure. Israeli strikes destroyed Sanaa airport, Hodeidah port, and power plants. The Houthis continued. Their weapons systems are mobile, dispersed, and cheap relative to the interceptors used against them. A Houthi drone costs a few thousand dollars to produce. An intercept missile costs $1–4 million. At scale, the Houthis could impose costs on the coalition indefinitely.

The structural insight — which Crisis Group articulated in March 2025 — is that the Houthis have established a precedent: a non-state actor in one of the world's poorest countries can disrupt global trade indefinitely. The solution is not primarily military. It requires resolving the political conditions — Gaza, Yemen's civil war, US-Iran tensions — that give the Houthis both the motivation and the domestic legitimacy to operate.

Leadership
The surviving leadership after Israel's decapitation campaign

Israel's campaign against Houthi leadership in 2025 was the most sustained assassination campaign against a non-state actor outside the Israel-Palestine conflict in the modern era. It destroyed the Houthi government's entire cabinet, killed the military chief of staff, and destroyed the missile unit commander. Abdul-Malik al-Houthi, the supreme leader, has survived. The movement has not collapsed.

Resilience note

The Houthi movement has survived the killing of its founder (Hussein al-Houthi, 2004), six wars with the Yemeni government, a decade of Saudi-led coalition airstrikes, and now Israel's decapitation campaign and the destruction of its governing cabinet.

It has not collapsed after any of these blows. The movement's resilience is structural: it is a mass movement with deep roots in Zaydi Shia communities in northwestern Yemen, not a personality cult or a hierarchical organization that depends on a single leader. Abdul-Malik al-Houthi is the supreme authority — but the movement would likely survive his death.

This is the central reason why military campaigns against the Houthis have produced limited strategic results despite significant tactical success.

Founder — Hussein al-Houthi

The movement takes its name from Hussein Badreddin al-Houthi (1959–2004), who founded the Believing Youth organization in Saada in the 1990s. Initially a parliamentary deputy, he shifted toward religious revivalism and eventually armed opposition to the Yemeni government.

He was killed in September 2004 by Yemeni government forces during the first Saada war. His death transformed the movement — he became a martyr and his younger brother Abdul-Malik became the new leader. The movement adopted his family name as its identity.

Truce holding · Indirect engagement
Operation Prosperity Guardian

The US-led multinational naval coalition deployed to protect Red Sea shipping from Houthi attacks. 15+ nations contributed. The operation successfully intercepted many incoming Houthi missiles and drones — but failed to deter continued attacks. The Houthis demonstrated that interception doesn't stop the campaign if you have enough weapons and tolerate enough losses.

Why it didn't work: The cost asymmetry. A Houthi drone: ~$5,000–$20,000 to build. A US SM-2 interceptor: ~$2.1 million. A SM-3: ~$10.8 million. Houthis can force the US to spend $10 million to stop a $10,000 drone. This is not a viable attrition ratio.

The Oman channel

Oman has served as the consistent backchannel between the United States and the Houthis — brokering the May 2025 ceasefire that stopped US strikes in exchange for Houthis not targeting US ships. This arrangement is geopolitically unusual: a designated FTO negotiating a sector-specific ceasefire with the country that designated them.

The Oman channel reflects the Sultanate's historic role as a neutral mediator in Gulf disputes — it maintains relations with Iran, the US, Israel, and the Houthis simultaneously.

Designations
How the world's governments classify Ansar Allah

Unlike Hezbollah, the Houthis do not have a well-established split between "political" and "military" wings in foreign government designations. Most designations cover the organization as a whole. The designations matter practically: FTO designation makes financial dealings illegal and complicates humanitarian operations in Houthi-controlled territory, where 19.5 million people need humanitarian assistance.

On the FTO designation and humanitarian access

The US FTO designation has a specific practical consequence that is widely noted by humanitarian organizations: it makes financial dealings with entities in Houthi-controlled territory legally risky. Since the Houthis control the territory where ~19.5 million people need assistance, and since aid operations must often coordinate with Houthi authorities to operate, the designation creates compliance risk for NGOs.

The World Food Programme beginning to end operations in Houthi-controlled areas in late 2025 — citing security risks and funding cuts — is one consequence. Some analysts argue the FTO designation, while symbolically important, functionally harms Yemeni civilians more than the Houthis themselves.

This is an active civic debate. FORA documents the argument without endorsing a conclusion.

What the Houthis have done — documented

FORA documents what organizations do. This tab records the Houthi factual record without characterizing it as legitimate or illegitimate — that determination belongs to the discourse system and to each user's judgment.

Red Sea attacks on commercial shipping
100+ vessels struck from Oct 2023 to late 2024. Two vessels sunk (Magic Seas, Eternity C) in July 2025. Crew members killed, injured, and detained. HRW has called for investigation as war crimes. The Russell Group estimated $1 trillion in trade disrupted Oct 2023–May 2024. The Houthis claim these attacks are limited to vessels with Israeli connections — this claim is disputed and several struck vessels had no documented Israeli connection.
Arbitrary detention of UN and aid workers
Since May 2024, 69 UN employees, NGO staff, and foreign embassy workers detained. Many denied family contact and legal access. The Houthis accuse them of espionage. HRW classifies the detentions as enforced disappearances. The Galaxy Leader cargo crew was detained for 14+ months.
Child soldier recruitment
Documented increase in child recruitment in 2025, framed as "defending Palestine." HRW and UN Panel of Experts have documented this pattern throughout the civil war. The Houthis deny recruiting children; documentation from multiple independent sources contradicts this.
Restriction of women's movement
Houthis have imposed mahram requirements — women must travel with a male relative or provide written male guardian approval. HRW documents increasing restrictions on women's freedom of movement across Houthi-controlled territories in 2025.
Obstruction of humanitarian aid
Houthis impose bureaucratic barriers on humanitarian organizations operating in their territory. Movement restrictions and access requirements constrain aid delivery to 19.5 million people in need. The WFP is ending operations in Houthi areas citing security risks and funding cuts.
Imprisonment · Religious persecution · Year 10
Levi Marhabi — The last Jew of Yemen
Levi Salem Musa Marhabi, born ~1987, is the last known Jew in Yemen. He was arrested by Houthi intelligence services in March 2016 after photographs emerged of him with Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu and an 800-year-old Torah scroll that had been removed from Yemen. The charge: smuggling a national treasure. He was sentenced to three years and six months in March 2018.

A Houthi appeals court ordered his release in September 2019, ruling he was innocent and wrongfully detained. The Houthis released his three Muslim co-defendants. They did not release him. The US State Department, senior rabbis, and the American Sephardi Federation have repeatedly called for his release. He remains imprisoned.

In March 2021, the Houthis expelled the 13 remaining Jews of Sanaa — including Marhabi's own mother — as a condition of a deal to free him. One of the expelled Jews said: "They gave us a choice between staying in the midst of harassment and keeping Salem a prisoner or leaving and having him released." The Houthis expelled them. They did not release him.

As of September 2024 he is in solitary confinement. He is reportedly partially paralyzed from a stroke. He has lost all his teeth from repeated torture. After October 7, 2023, the Houthis moved him to a more dangerous prison ward with extremist prisoners who threatened him.

The UN confirmed in 2022 that only one Jew remained in Yemen. Yemen's 2,500-year-old Jewish community — which once numbered 50,000 before Operation Magic Carpet in 1949–50 — has been reduced to one man, imprisoned by the men whose battle cry includes "curse the Jews."

They frame women's movement restrictions as cultural/religious protection. FORA records the framing. The factual record above also stands.

Houthi Control — Land vs. Population
~34M Yemenis · Three ways to see the same data
The critical distinction: The Houthis control ~15–20% of Yemen's land area — but that land holds ~70% of its people. Yemen's ~34 million people are concentrated in the northwest highlands, Sanaa, and the Red Sea coast. The government controls vast, empty desert.

Why this gap exists
Yemen's western highlands and Red Sea coast — Houthi territory — are the agricultural heartland and historically the most densely settled region. The eastern interior (held by the internationally recognized government) is largely desert. Marib governorate, the most contested zone, has oil wealth but relatively low population. The Houthis fought their way to the places people live.
