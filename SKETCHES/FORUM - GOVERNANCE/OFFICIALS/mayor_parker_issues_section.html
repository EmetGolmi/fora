Kensington drug & homelessness crisis
High tension
×
Enforcement side
Clear the encampments. The open-air drug market is a public safety disaster. Kensington residents deserve to live without needles in front of their doors. Enforcement first — treatment is available for those who want it.
vs
Harm reduction side
Sweeps don't solve addiction — they displace it. People need housing, treatment, and safe consumption before criminalization. Clearing encampments without adequate shelter kills people.
Parker's position
Enforcement-first with treatment offered. Declared a public safety emergency in Kensington on Day 1. Cleared the Kensington Ave encampment in May 2024. Opposed safe injection sites. Signed EO adding 1,000 new shelter beds (Dec 2025). Invested $12M+ in anti-violence and overdose prevention.
What's happened
Jan 2024
Parker declares Kensington public safety emergency on first day in office
May 2024
First large-scale encampment sweep — 59 of ~75 residents accepted shelter or treatment
Sep 2024
Second sweep — 34 arrested. One person, Amanda Cahill, dies in jail three days later
Feb 2025
City reports: crime down 17%, homicides down 45%, shootings down 57% in Kensington district
Dec 2025
EO signed adding 1,000 shelter beds. Parker says open-air drug market "not done yet" but improving
What critics say
Drug use and homelessness haven't gone away — they've moved to surrounding blocks and other neighborhoods
Parker proposed cutting $1M from Prevention Point, a harm reduction and syringe exchange program
Amanda Cahill's death in custody after a sweep arrest raised serious questions about the city's approach
Treatment capacity hasn't kept pace with the number of people swept off the streets
Sources: Philadelphia Inquirer · PhillyVoice · Billy Penn · City of Philadelphia press releases · Wikipedia · as of March 2026
ICE & immigration enforcement
High tension
×
Fight harder side
Parker should declare Philly a sanctuary city loudly and fight the Trump administration directly. ~47,000 undocumented residents need clear protection — silence is dangerous.
vs
Stay quiet side
Philly receives $2.8B in federal grants. Picking a public fight risks losing that money. The 2016 policy stays in place regardless — quiet protection beats loud punishment.
Parker's position
"Welcoming city" — not "sanctuary city." The 2016 Kenney executive order remains in place: city jails won't honor ICE detainers without a judicial warrant, and only for violent felony convictions. Parker has deliberately avoided confrontation with the Trump administration. In Aug 2025 the city secretly told the DOJ it had no plans to change its policies — but tried to keep the letter private.
What's happened
Nov 2024
Parker declines to reassert "sanctuary city" label after Trump's election. "I don't know what will happen."
Dec 2024
Dozens rally at City Hall demanding Parker take a firm stand. Her office declines further comment.
Jan 2025
ICE raids car wash in Philly — 7 arrested. City does not intervene.
Aug 2025
City Solicitor sends secret letter to DOJ affirming "welcoming city" policies won't change. City tries to block its release under RTK law.
Feb 2026
Inquirer obtains letter via open records. Council members Landau and Brooks propose "ICE Out" bills — a veto-proof majority signs on.
What critics say
Immigrant advocates say Parker's cautiousness leaves ~47,000 undocumented residents uncertain of their safety
The secret DOJ letter — and attempt to hide it — undermined trust in Parker's transparency
City Council is moving further than Parker on this — the "ICE Out" proposal has a veto-proof majority
Parker's former Office of Immigrant Affairs director resigned, saying they "weren't 100% on the same page"
Sources: Philadelphia Inquirer · NBC10 · Billy Penn · Axios Philadelphia · CBS Philadelphia · as of March 2026
Schools & extended year
Active debate
×
Pro-extended year
Summer learning loss is real, especially for low-income students. Working parents need affordable childcare. More school time closes the opportunity gap. Year-round schooling works in other cities.
vs
Critics say
Parker promised "year-round school" on the campaign trail — what she delivered is before/after care at 40 schools. The academic calendar hasn't changed. This is rebranded childcare, not education reform.
Parker's position
"Extended Day, Extended Year" — not year-round school. 40 schools (20 public + 5 charters in Year 1, 15 more added Year 2). Schools open 7:30am–6pm, including winter/spring breaks and 6-week summer programs. Voluntary for families. Parker has walked back the "year-round" label but calls the program an essential opportunity gap-closer.
What's happened
Campaign
Parker promises mandatory year-round schooling — a signature pledge
Jun 2024
Launches pilot at 25 schools. Rebranded as "Extended Day, Extended Year" — voluntary, not year-round
Oct 2024
Reporting shows pilot mostly expanded access to existing programs already running at schools
Jun 2025
Expanded to 40 schools. ~3,000 students participating. Parker demurs on whether full year-round is coming
What critics say
Campaign promise was "year-round school" — what was delivered is before/after care with a new name
In Year 1, programming was largely outsourced to nonprofits that had been operating in these schools for years anyway
The Philadelphia Federation of Teachers was not consulted before the rollout — schools were notified days before Parker's press conference
Only 3,000 of ~100,000+ Philly public school students are participating after two years
Sources: Philadelphia Inquirer · Chalkbeat Philadelphia · WHYY · 6abc · CBS Philadelphia · as of March 2026
