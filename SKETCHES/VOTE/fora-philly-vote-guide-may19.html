Pennsylvania · Statewide · 4-Year Term
Governor of Pennsylvania
The chief executive of Pennsylvania state government — sets the state budget, signs or vetoes every law, commands the National Guard, and appoints hundreds of judges and officials.
Uncontested Primary
▼
What this job actually is
The Governor is Pennsylvania's equivalent of a CEO — running a state government with 80,000+ employees and a $45+ billion annual budget. Every agency from PennDOT to the Department of Education to the state police reports up to the Governor. If something breaks in Pennsylvania, the Governor is accountable.
Real power this office holds
Signs or vetoes every bill the legislature passes. Controls the state budget proposal. Appoints state Supreme Court justices to fill vacancies. Activates the National Guard. Issues pardons. Can declare disaster emergencies, close schools, and mobilize state resources during crises.
Skills you'd require if you were hiring
Executive management of large organizations. Budget negotiation. Ability to work across party lines to pass legislation. Crisis leadership. Understanding of education, criminal justice, infrastructure, and healthcare policy. A track record you can verify.
Why this race matters to Philadelphia
The Governor controls how much state funding Philadelphia schools receive. The Governor appoints members of the Philadelphia school board. State police resources, highway funding, gun law enforcement — all run through the Governor's office. Philadelphia is the largest city in the state and gets significant resources from Harrisburg.
What power looks like in practice
Gov. Wolf used emergency powers to close Pennsylvania schools and businesses during COVID. Gov. Shapiro has used executive orders to protect abortion access, expand broadband, and redirect state funds to education. A Governor who vetoes school funding bills or expands charter schools can reshape education in Philadelphia for a generation. The Governor also sets PA's position on federal policy — infrastructure dollars, climate money, healthcare waivers all run through the Governor's office.
📋 Questions to ask before you vote for Governor
  • How has this person handled a real budget or large organization? What was the outcome?
  • What is their position on Philadelphia school funding — how much and through what mechanism?
  • Have they supported or opposed legislation that affects gun policy, policing, or housing in Pennsylvania?
  • Who are their top campaign donors? What industries are bankrolling this campaign?
  • What is their record on reproductive rights, given Pennsylvania's role as a refuge state for abortion access?

Pennsylvania · Statewide · 4-Year Term
Lieutenant Governor
The Governor's second-in-command. Steps in if the Governor can't serve. Presides over the state Senate and chairs several important boards.
D: Uncontested · R: 2 Candidates
▼
What this job actually is
Think of the Lt. Governor as a deputy who also has their own job. In Pennsylvania, the Lt. Governor presides over the state Senate (can cast tie-breaking votes), chairs the Board of Pardons (reviews clemency applications for people in prison), and heads the Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency (PEMA). They also take over as Governor if the Governor resigns, is removed, or dies in office.
Real power this office holds
Board of Pardons chair — significant power over who gets released from prison or has their record cleared. Presides over the PA Senate and can cast tie-breaking votes. Emergency management oversight. Full Governor powers if needed. The Lt. Governor position is often an important stepping stone — Austin Davis is widely expected to run for Governor in 2030.
Skills you'd require if you were hiring
Political experience, legislative knowledge, ability to manage complex government functions, steady crisis leadership, and a public record you can examine. Their position on criminal justice reform matters given the Board of Pardons role.
Why it matters to Philadelphia
Philadelphia has the highest incarceration rate of any large American city. The Lt. Governor chairing the Board of Pardons means they have real influence over clemency decisions that affect Philadelphians inside the state prison system. Emergency management also touches Philadelphia directly during disasters or public health crises.
📋 Questions to ask before you vote for Lt. Governor
  • What is their stated position on criminal justice reform and clemency — who should get a second chance?
  • Have they worked with or in state government before? What specifically did they accomplish?
  • Do they align with the Governor they'd serve under? A Lt. Governor who clashes with the Governor creates paralysis.

Federal · 2-Year Term · Philadelphia has 3 Congressional Districts
U.S. Representative (Congress)
Your member of the U.S. House of Representatives. Votes on every federal law — taxes, healthcare, immigration, military spending, foreign aid. This is the most direct line between Philadelphia and Washington.
3rd District: 4-Way Dem Primary
▼
What this job actually is
Your U.S. Representative is one of 435 members of the House of Representatives. They vote on every federal law — including the federal budget, healthcare legislation, immigration policy, foreign aid, and gun laws. They also sit on committees that oversee specific areas of government. A single representative can introduce legislation, hold hearings, and bring federal dollars back to their district.
Real power this office holds
Votes on all federal legislation. Controls federal appropriations through committee work — earmarks for Philadelphia projects come through here. Oversight of federal agencies including ICE, the VA, the FDA, and more. Confirms or blocks key executive appointees through committees. The margin in the House is thin — one representative's vote can determine whether legislation passes.
Skills you'd require if you were hiring
Legislative experience or deep policy expertise. A track record of constituent services — helping people navigate federal agencies. Ability to work in coalition. Clear positions on federal issues. Willingness to show up, attend votes, and hold town halls. Committee assignment ambition — the most powerful reps chair committees.
Which district are you in?
District 2: Northeast Philadelphia and parts of North Philadelphia east of Broad St. (Brendan Boyle, D, uncontested)
District 3: West Philadelphia, Center City, parts of North Philly — open seat, contested 4-way primary replacing retiring Rep. Dwight Evans
District 5: Parts of Philadelphia including South Philadelphia, Southwest Philadelphia (Mary Gay Scanlon, D, uncontested) → Find your district
Why the 3rd District race is the one to watch
Rep. Dwight Evans is retiring after 40+ years in public service — 14 of them in Congress. This open seat covers some of Philadelphia's most diverse and densely populated neighborhoods: West Philadelphia, Cobbs Creek, Brewerytown, parts of North and Center City. Four Democrats are competing to replace him. Whoever wins the primary almost certainly wins in November — this district is heavily Democratic. This is the most consequential Philadelphia primary race tomorrow.
📋 Questions to ask before you vote for U.S. Representative
  • Have they actually passed legislation, or just introduced bills that went nowhere?
  • What is their position on ICE enforcement and immigration? Will they push back on federal overreach?
  • Where do they stand on the federal budget — specifically Medicaid, Social Security, and housing vouchers that affect Philadelphia?
  • What committee do they want to serve on — and does it match Philadelphia's biggest needs?
  • Who is funding their campaign? Federal PAC money can come from industries with business before Congress.
  • Do they support Palestinian human rights? This has become a litmus test issue in West Philadelphia specifically.
  • What has their relationship been with community organizations in the district?

Pennsylvania State Senator
Pennsylvania's upper legislative chamber. Passes state laws, confirms the Governor's appointments, and controls the state budget alongside the House. Half the Senate is on the ballot every two years.
2nd · 4th · 8th Districts
▼
What this job actually is
Pennsylvania has 50 state senators. They write and vote on state laws, confirm the Governor's appointments to state agencies and courts, and negotiate the state budget each year. A state senator has a larger constituency than a state rep but still represents a specific geographic area. They serve 4-year terms, so these choices last.
Real power this office holds
Votes on state law — everything from tenant protections to gun regulations to education funding formulas. Confirms judicial appointments. Budget power — the Senate must pass the annual state budget. Investigative committees can subpoena records and hold agencies accountable. Committee chairs hold especially concentrated power.
Skills you'd require if you were hiring
Legislative experience or deep expertise in a specific policy area. Track record of passing bills, not just introducing them. Constituent services history. Willingness to negotiate. Transparency about their positions on the big issues — school funding, housing, criminal justice, reproductive rights.
Which districts are in Philadelphia?

4th District: Contested — Art Haywood vs. Mike Cogbill (D). Covers parts of Montgomery County and Philadelphia's northwest.
8th District: Contested — Anthony Hardy Williams vs. David Goldsmith Jr. (D). Covers parts of West Philadelphia and Delaware County.
📋 Questions to ask before you vote for State Senator
  • What bills have they actually passed — not just sponsored — during their time in office?
  • How do they vote on education funding for Philadelphia schools vs. charter schools?
  • What is their position on rent control, eviction protections, and housing investment?
  • Have they voted consistently with their stated positions, or do they vote differently once the cameras are off?
  • What is their relationship with the local Democratic Party organization? Are they independent or a machine candidate?

Pennsylvania · 2-Year Term · 20+ Philadelphia Districts on Ballot
Pennsylvania State Representative
Your closest link to state government. Represents your neighborhood in Harrisburg. Votes on every state law — housing, schools, wages, criminal justice. All 203 seats are up every two years.
Some Contested Primaries
▼
What this job actually is
Pennsylvania has 203 state representatives — one for roughly every 65,000 residents. They write state laws, vote on the state budget, and provide direct constituent services to people in their district. Your state rep's office can help you navigate state agencies — unemployment, driver's licenses, state benefits, and state contracts. They're also the people you call when a local issue needs a state response.
Real power this office holds
Votes on all state legislation. Democrats currently hold a one-seat majority — every seat matters. Committee work shapes which bills even get a vote. Constituent services — your rep's office intervenes with state agencies on your behalf. District resources — state grants and projects are often directed by your rep. Local presence you can actually access.
Skills you'd require if you were hiring
Community presence and accessibility. Knowledge of local issues. Ability to navigate Harrisburg — committee work and coalition-building matter more than speeches. A track record of showing up — attendance records, town halls, response to constituent requests. Clear positions on housing, schools, public safety, and workers' rights.
How to find your district
Philadelphia has roughly 25 state house districts. Your district is determined by your home address. Use the PA voter registration lookup or the PA Legislature District Finder → to find your specific representative and who is on your primary ballot. Some districts have contested primaries — those are listed by ward on the official candidates page.

Party Internal · PA Democratic / Republican State Committee
Member of State Committee
The internal governing body of the political party. These people shape the party's platform, endorse candidates, and control party resources. Most voters have never heard of this office. It matters more than you think.
Multiple Contested Races
▼
What this job actually is
State committee members are the internal board of directors for the Democratic or Republican Party in Pennsylvania. They set the party's platform, vote on party endorsements, control party funds, and make party rules. These are not government positions — you don't get a government salary. But they shape who the party supports and how it operates. The committee votes on party chair and can influence what issues the party prioritizes.
Real power this office holds
Party endorsements — committee members collectively decide which candidates the party backs in competitive primaries. Party platform — they approve the positions the party officially takes. Party chair election — they vote for the state party chair, who manages field operations and messaging. Rules changes — can reform or entrench how the party operates. A progressive bloc on the committee can push the party left; a machine bloc can protect incumbents.
Why most voters skip this and why they shouldn't
These positions are filled by political insiders when ordinary voters don't show up. Progressive candidates and reform-minded citizens who win state committee seats can shift how the Democratic Party operates in Philadelphia and Pennsylvania — who it endorses, what it fights for, how it spends money. Machine politicians fill these seats when nobody else runs. The people who control the party infrastructure often have more long-term influence than any single elected official. If you care about reforming the party from within, this is where you do it.
📋 Questions to ask
  • Are they an insider protecting the status quo or someone pushing for party reform?
  • What specific change to party operations or platform do they want to make?
  • Who recruited them to run — ward leaders, reform organizations, or did they come up themselves?
  • Check endorsements: which state committee candidates are backed by reform groups like Reclaim Philadelphia, Philadelphia DSA, or the ward machine?

Hyper-Local · Party Internal · Elected by Division
Ward Executive Committee Member
The most local political position that exists. These are the people who staff polling places, knock on doors, and decide who the ward endorses. Most seats go uncontested because nobody runs.
Often Uncontested
▼
What this job actually is
Ward committee members are the ground-level organizers of the political party. Each ward is divided into divisions (precincts), and each division elects 2 committee members. They staff their polling place on election day, do get-out-the-vote work, and collectively the ward committee votes on who to endorse in local races. The committee also elects the ward leader — one of the most powerful local political figures in Philadelphia.
Real power this office holds
Ward endorsements — committee members vote on who the ward backs. In heavily Democratic Philadelphia wards, an endorsement can determine who wins a local race. Election day operations — they run the polling place and manage turnout. Ward leader selection — the committee elects the ward leader, who has significant patronage and political power. Party gatekeeping — machine-controlled wards can freeze out challengers; reform committee members can break that open.
The most important thing to know
Most of these seats go uncontested because nobody runs. In many Philadelphia divisions, you can run for ward committee and win with just a handful of write-in votes. If you or someone you know wants to build political power from the ground up, running for ward committee is one of the most effective and underused strategies available to ordinary citizens. Reform organizations like Reclaim Philadelphia have deliberately targeted ward committee seats to reshape who controls the party machine.

Philadelphia Only
Ballot Questions
These questions appear on every Philadelphia voter's ballot — regardless of party registration. Independents and third-party voters who can't vote on candidates CAN vote on these questions. Read them carefully. They change city law permanently.
Question 1
Should Philadelphia create a city-run retirement savings program?
Plain language: This question asks whether the City of Philadelphia should create and administer a retirement savings program for workers whose employers don't offer one. If approved, the city would set up an automatic enrollment retirement account system — workers could opt out, but would be automatically enrolled. Small businesses and gig workers who currently have no 401(k) or pension would gain access to a city-managed retirement savings vehicle.
What YES means: The city creates the program and can require certain employers to participate. Workers get automatic access to a retirement savings account.
What NO means: No city-run program. Workers without employer-sponsored retirement benefits continue to have no automatic option.
Who supports / who opposes
Supported by labor unions and progressive advocacy groups who argue too many Philadelphia workers — particularly in service industries — have no retirement savings at all. Some small business groups have raised concerns about administrative burden. The program would be voluntary for workers but would require city oversight infrastructure.
Question 2
Should the city make permanent the office that monitors youth services?
Plain language: Philadelphia has an office that monitors programs serving children and youth — foster care, juvenile justice, youth development, and related services. This question asks whether that oversight office should be made permanent in the city charter, rather than existing only at the discretion of the administration.
What YES means: The youth services oversight office is protected by the charter — future administrations cannot eliminate it without a new vote of the people.
What NO means: The office remains subject to being defunded or eliminated by future mayors without a public vote.
Context
Philadelphia has faced significant scrutiny over child welfare failures. Making an oversight body permanent in the charter means it can't be quietly eliminated under budget pressure or political convenience. Charter amendments require voter approval to undo, creating a structural accountability guarantee.
