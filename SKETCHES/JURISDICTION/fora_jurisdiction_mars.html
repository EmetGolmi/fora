Overview
The planet we're about to change — before we're ready

Mars is 346 million kilometers away today. A signal takes 19 minutes to reach it. Two robotic rovers are currently driving across its surface — one of them sitting next to a rock that a 2025 study concluded was most likely shaped by microbial excretion. A January 2026 study confirmed that Jezero Crater once had a real shoreline, wave-lapped, where liquid water pooled for long enough that life could have taken hold. The samples Perseverance has collected over five years may contain the most important scientific cargo ever assembled. Whether those samples come home — and when — is the most consequential decision in planetary science history, and it will be made this year.

Mars is also, simultaneously, a future jurisdiction being contested before it is occupied. SpaceX announced in February 2026 that it is delaying its Mars ambitions 5–7 years to focus on the Moon. NASA's human Mars roadmap is in place but unfunded at mission level. China's Tianwen-3 robotic sample return is in spacecraft construction. The next crewed mission transfer window opens in late 2028. The question of who governs a Mars settlement, what legal system applies to colonists 350 million kilometers from Earth, and whether the OST's non-sovereignty clause can be enforced across interplanetary distances is entirely unresolved.

What makes Mars uniquely urgent among FORA jurisdictions: the decisions being made now — about sample return, about the biosignature at Cheyava Falls, about whether to confirm or postpone the life question — are irreversible in both directions. If humans land on Mars before we answer whether Mars harbors life, we contaminate Mars with Earth microbes forever. If we wait too long, the political window for an international governance framework closes and facts on the ground are set by whoever arrives first under whatever rules they choose to recognize.

Iron oxide — why it's red
The Martian surface is covered in fine iron oxide (rust) dust blown across the entire planet by global dust storms. The "sky" appears butterscotch to pinkish-orange from the surface. During dust storms, which can last months and cover the entire planet, skies turn dark brown. The dust also coats solar panels — it's what killed the Opportunity rover in 2018.
Two moons
Phobos — Fear (Φόβος)
Only 27 km across. Orbits Mars in just 7 hours 39 minutes — faster than Mars rotates. Rises in the west and sets in the east. Slowly spiraling inward — will either crash into Mars or break apart into a ring system in ~50 million years. Already shows surface grooves possibly from tidal stress.
Deimos — Dread (Δεῖμος)
Only 15 km across. Smoothest moon in the Solar System — craters filled with fine regolith. So small that from the Martian surface it appears as just a bright star. Takes 30 hours to orbit — so it rises and sets like a slow moon.
Geography
A world of records — tallest volcano, deepest canyon, largest impact basin

Mars has a total surface area of 144.4 million km² — approximately equal to all the dry land on Earth. Despite being half Earth's size, the planet's lack of oceans means its entire surface is accessible terrain. The Martian landscape is defined by extreme dichotomy: smooth, low-lying plains in the north (mostly younger volcanic material) and heavily cratered highlands in the south, thousands of meters higher in elevation. The cause of this north-south divide — the Martian Dichotomy — remains one of the fundamental unsolved questions in planetary science.

Mars holds the tallest volcano in the Solar System (Olympus Mons at 21.9 km, nearly three times Everest), the longest canyon system (Valles Marineris, 4,000 km long — wide enough to cross the continental United States), and the largest impact basin (Hellas Planitia, 7,152 km wide and 9 km deep). The planet is geologically active — marsquakes are regularly detected, and the enormous Tharsis Bulge, a volcanic plateau the size of North America, has deformed the entire Martian crust with its mass.

Key features
Olympus Mons — 21.9 km tall
The tallest volcano and highest point in the Solar System. ~600 km across at its base — so large that from its summit, the horizon curves below the base. The escarpment at its edge drops 8 km vertically. Likely still geologically active within the last 25 million years.
Valles Marineris — 4,000 km long
The Solar System's largest canyon system. Up to 7 km deep, 200 km wide. If placed on Earth it would stretch from Los Angeles to New York. Formed primarily by tectonic rifting and collapse — not water erosion, though water may have flowed through it.
Hellas Planitia — deepest basin
7,152 km wide impact basin, 9 km below average surface elevation. Atmospheric pressure at its floor is nearly double the planetary average — the closest Mars gets to conditions where liquid water could theoretically persist on the surface today.
Jezero Crater — active science site
45 km wide ancient lake crater. Site of an ancient river delta confirmed to have harbored a lake for potentially millions of years. Perseverance's landing site. A 2026 study confirmed a wave-lapped shoreline within the crater — real beach geology. The most astrobiologically promising site ever explored on Mars.
Polar ice caps
Both poles have permanent water ice caps covered seasonally by CO₂ (dry ice) frost. The north polar cap is ~1.6 km thick, ~1,000 km wide. Radar data from Mars Express and MRO suggests large subsurface liquid water lakes beneath the south polar ice — potentially habitable niches today.
The Martian dichotomy

Mars is split hemispheres. The northern hemisphere is smooth, low, and relatively young volcanic plains — the "lowlands." The southern hemisphere is ancient, heavily cratered, and sits an average 3–5 km higher — the "highlands." The boundary between them is one of the sharpest topographic discontinuities of any planet.

Two main theories: (1) a massive impact in the early Solar System removed the northern crust (the "giant impact" hypothesis); (2) convection patterns in the early Martian mantle thinned the northern crust naturally. Neither is definitively proven. The MAVEN and InSight missions have provided data constraining both, without settling the question.

Water — then and now
3–4 billion years ago
Mars had rivers, lakes, possibly a northern ocean. Evidence: valley networks, delta deposits, lake sediments, orbital mineral signatures of water-altered rocks across vast regions.
Today — surface
No stable liquid water on the surface. Atmospheric pressure too low — water either evaporates or freezes instantly. CO₂ and water ice at poles. Seasonal frost. Possible brine flows (dark streaks on crater walls — still debated).
Today — subsurface
InSight found that 11.5–20 km below the Martian surface, the crust is saturated with liquid water. If confirmed, this represents the largest known reservoir of liquid water on Mars — a potential habitat for extant microbial life today.

Science & Life
The most important question in science — and we're close to answering it

Did life ever exist on Mars? Does it exist there now? These are not rhetorical questions — they have answers, the answers exist in physical material, and some of that material may currently be sitting in titanium tubes aboard Perseverance. We have never been closer to knowing. We have also never been closer to contaminating the question permanently.

Cheyava Falls — the leopard rock
The strongest biosignature candidate ever found off Earth

In July 2024, Perseverance discovered the "Cheyava Falls" rock on the edge of an ancient river valley in Jezero Crater. The rock is light-colored with vivid reddish-brown leopard-spot patterns — irregular patches of iron and phosphate minerals surrounded by halos of whitish calcium sulfate.

On Earth, these patterns are commonly produced by microbial colonies oxidizing iron for energy — a process called chemolithotrophy. Organisms called iron-oxidizing bacteria leave behind exactly these mineral signatures as metabolic byproducts. Abiotic (non-biological) processes can produce similar-looking patterns through water-rock chemistry, but they tend to be more regular, more uniform, and less precisely structured than what Cheyava Falls shows.

A 2025 peer-reviewed study analyzing the rock concluded that microbial excretion is the most likely explanation for the leopard-spot pattern — ahead of the most plausible abiotic mechanisms. This is not a confirmed detection of life. It is a statement that the biological hypothesis fits the data better than the alternatives we have tested so far. The only way to know for certain is to analyze the samples in Earth-based laboratories with instruments far more powerful than anything a rover can carry. Those samples are in tubes on Perseverance's belly right now.

The ancient beach — January 2026
Jezero once had a real shoreline

A January 2026 study published in JGR Planets by a team led by Imperial College London analyzed the geology of Jezero Crater's Margin Unit — a band of rock lining the inner edge of the crater that Perseverance explored in 2023–24. The findings: the Margin Unit is a paleoshoreline. The rocks show wave-generated structures, layered beach deposits, and mineral signatures consistent with prolonged immersion in shallow standing water.

This matters because it pushes back the window for Martian life significantly. Lakes can form and dry in geologically short periods. Shorelines require sustained water over much longer timescales — the kind of stable, persistent aquatic environment where life as we know it not only originates but has time to become complex. Jezero wasn't a flash flood or a transient melt event. It had a beach. It had seasons. It had time.

Mars Sample Return — the decision of 2026
The most important scientific decision of the decade

Perseverance has sealed 23 of its 43 sample tubes. The sealed tubes contain material from some of the most astrobiologically significant sites ever sampled on Mars, including the Cheyava Falls biosignature rock, delta sediments from the ancient lake floor, and igneous rocks from Jezero's volcanic basement that provide a geological timeline of the crater's history.

Bringing them to Earth requires a Mars Sample Return (MSR) mission — the most complex interplanetary mission ever attempted. After NASA's original MSR plan ballooned to an estimated $11 billion and faced Congressional cancellation, NASA identified two streamlined alternatives: one using the proven sky-crane landing system ($6.6–7.7B) and one leveraging SpaceX Starship ($5.8–7.1B). Both aim for Earth return as early as 2035. A decision between these alternatives is expected mid-2026.

China's Tianwen-3, now in spacecraft construction, is targeting a simpler "grab-and-go" sample return from a single accessible surface site — it could potentially bring Martian material home before NASA's curated samples, though from a less scientifically selected location. The geopolitical and scientific stakes of who returns the first Martian samples — and what those samples contain — are without precedent in the history of space exploration.

Planetary protection · the contamination clock

Article IX of the Outer Space Treaty requires states to "avoid harmful contamination" of celestial bodies. NASA's Planetary Protection Office classifies Mars as Category IV — the second-highest protection level, reserved for bodies where conditions may support Earth life and that may harbor extant life.

Every Mars lander is sterilized to strict microbial limits before launch. Rovers are built in ISO-certified clean rooms. Yet none of this is sufficient for a crewed mission. Humans carry trillions of microorganisms — in their gut, on their skin, in their respiratory tract. A crewed Mars landing is, from a planetary protection standpoint, an extinction-level event for any indigenous Martian microbial ecosystem.

The irreversibility is absolute: once humans land on Mars, it is permanently and irrecoverably contaminated with Earth life. If Mars harbors indigenous microbes, we will never be able to distinguish them from human-introduced contamination with any certainty. The life question becomes permanently ambiguous. Some scientists argue this means we must confirm or deny Martian life before any crewed mission. Others argue that certainty may never come, and that human presence is the only way to explore at sufficient depth. There is no scientific consensus and no legal framework for resolving it.

Missions · History
From Mariner to Perseverance — 60 years of exploring Mars
1964
Mariner 4 — first successful Mars flyby. 22 photographs. Mars is cratered and cold — not the canal-crossed world of 19th-century imagination. Atmospheric pressure only 1% of Earth's. The dream of Martian civilization ends here.
1971
Mariner 9 — first spacecraft to orbit another planet. Arrives during a global dust storm — waits two months for the dust to clear. Maps 85% of the surface. Discovers Olympus Mons, Valles Marineris, and ancient riverbeds. Mars has geological complexity — and a past with water.
1976
Viking 1 and 2 land successfully. Conducted biology experiments — including the Labeled Release experiment, which produced a positive result that the mission's chief experimenter, Gilbert Levin, maintained until his death was evidence of life. The scientific consensus declared the results likely chemical, not biological. The ambiguity never fully resolved.
1997
Pathfinder and Sojourner — first rover on Mars. Proves surface mobility is feasible. Returns 16,500 images and data on 15 chemical analyses of rocks. Sojourner drives ~100 meters total. The era of rovers begins.
2004
Spirit and Opportunity land 3 weeks apart. Spirit operates 6 years; Opportunity, 15 years (died in the 2018 global dust storm). Opportunity drives 45.16 km — a record that Perseverance is approaching. Both confirm Mars was once wet enough to sustain liquid water for extended periods.
2012
Curiosity lands in Gale Crater. Car-sized rover, still operating. Detects organic molecules in rocks. Detects seasonal methane fluctuations — possibly biological, possibly geological. Confirms Mars was habitable to microbial life in the past. Still driving as of March 2026.
2021
Perseverance lands in Jezero Crater (Feb 18). Ingenuity helicopter makes the first powered flight on another planet (Apr 19). UAE Hope orbiter and China's Tianwen-1/Zhurong all arrive in the same window — the most missions to Mars in a single launch window in history. The international character of Mars exploration is established.
2024–26
Perseverance crosses 40 km driven, surpassing Curiosity. First AI-planned rover drives (Dec 2025). Cheyava Falls biosignature rock analyzed. Ancient beach confirmed at Jezero (Jan 2026). Mars Sample Return decision pending. Next opposition: Feb 2027.

Legal Status
Mars has one law, written before anyone was going there, and it has four fatal gaps

The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 applies to Mars exactly as it applies to the Moon: no sovereignty claims by any means, peaceful use only, states responsible for their nationals' activities. Mars is explicitly covered — the treaty covers "the Moon and other celestial bodies." These provisions are binding on all 118 parties, including the US, Russia, and China.

Beyond the OST, there is no Mars-specific law. Not a treaty, not a framework, not a set of agreed norms. The Artemis Accords mention Mars in their scope — their provisions on heritage protection, safety zones, and resource extraction extend explicitly to Mars — but they are non-binding political commitments and Russia and China have not signed them. The Moon Agreement (1979) applies to Mars in theory but has been ratified by none of the major spacefaring nations.

The four gaps that matter
Gap 1 · Signal delay makes Earth governance structurally impossible
19 minutes one way, 38 minutes round trip at current distance. This is a physics problem, not a policy problem. Any governance system for Mars must be local by necessity — Earth cannot supervise or instruct in real time. The OST's framework of state responsibility for nationals assumes the state can respond. On Mars, it cannot.
Gap 2 · No resource extraction framework
As with the Moon, OST Article II forbids sovereignty over Mars. The US Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act (2015) asserts Americans can own extracted resources without claiming the planet. But Mars contains water ice, perchlorates, carbon dioxide convertible to rocket fuel, and potentially rare minerals. No international framework governs who can extract what, where, or under what terms.
Gap 3 · Citizenship, succession, and legal personhood of Martian-born humans
A person born on Mars to American parents is — what? A US citizen by birthright citizenship? A citizen of no recognized state? What legal system governs their rights, their contracts, their disputes? No nation has addressed this. International law has no answer. The first Martian-born human will be a legal anomaly with no existing framework.
Gap 4 · The independence threshold
SpaceX's stated position: when a Mars settlement is self-sustaining, it should be self-governing. At what population? What economic threshold? What legal trigger? The OST does not contemplate the transition from exploration to settlement to self-determination. No legal framework does. The question of when Mars stops being a territory of Earth states and becomes its own political entity has no answer in existing law.
The SpaceX Starlink clause

In 2021, SpaceX's Starlink terms of service included the following clause for services provided on Mars:

"For services provided on Mars, or in transit to Mars via Starship or other colonization spacecraft, the parties recognize Mars as a free planet and that no Earth-based government has authority or sovereignty over Martian activities."

This is a Terms of Service clause, not a law. No government agreed to it. No court has evaluated it. No international body has recognized it. But it is the clearest public statement of SpaceX's intended legal position on Mars governance — and it was published three years before any Mars mission was imminent, as a deliberate legal signal.

Planetary protection law

OST Article IX requires avoiding "harmful contamination" of celestial bodies. NASA has formal Category IV planetary protection protocols for Mars — the second-highest protection level. These are implemented through domestic US law and NASA policy.

The legal question: does landing humans on Mars — which inevitably and irreversibly introduces Earth microbes — violate Article IX? If Mars harbors extant life, arguably yes. If it doesn't, arguably no. The answer depends on a fact we don't yet know: whether Mars is currently alive.

No court has ever addressed this. No international enforcement mechanism for Article IX exists. If SpaceX landed humans on Mars tomorrow, the treaty violation — if it is one — could not be enforced by anyone against anyone. International space law has no compliance mechanism beyond diplomatic pressure and political cost.

The Colony Question
SpaceX, a million people, and the law of a world that doesn't exist yet

SpaceX's stated mission is to establish a self-sustaining human colony on Mars — ultimately of one million people, as insurance against human extinction on Earth. This is not a distant ambition: SpaceX has named the first Starship to go to Mars the "Heart of Gold" (after the spaceship in The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy), has published architecture documents for Mars colonization, and has built Starbase in Texas as the primary Mars launch facility. The program has been delayed — on February 9, 2026, Musk announced a 5–7 year delay to focus on the Moon. It has not been cancelled.

The governance problem is not primarily legal — it is physical. The 19-minute signal delay means that any dispute, emergency, or governance decision on Mars must be resolved locally. A medical emergency cannot wait 38 minutes for an Earth response. A criminal matter cannot be adjudicated by a court 350 million kilometers away. A political crisis cannot be resolved by a government that receives news of it 19 minutes after it happens. Whatever governs a Mars settlement, it must be local by necessity — not just by preference.

The democratic deficit is equally acute. The first Mars settlers would depend entirely on SpaceX for their means of survival: the spacecraft that brought them, the habitat that houses them, the ISRU equipment that produces their air and water and fuel. They cannot leave without SpaceX's help. They cannot be resupplied without SpaceX's help. The relationship between a corporation and a captive settlement entirely dependent on that corporation for survival is not analogous to any prior political arrangement in human history — including colonial ones, which at least could be physically left.

Historical parallels — and why they all fail

American colonies: Similar in the sense of physical distance from the governing power, gradual development of local governance, eventual independence. Different in that colonists could sail home, and the distance was weeks, not years. The signal delay alone makes Mars constitutionally unlike any colony in history.

Antarctica: The closest legal analog — an international commons governed by the Antarctic Treaty, with scientific stations but no permanent settlement. Mars could develop similarly if major powers agreed. The difference: no one has an economic or strategic incentive to colonize Antarctica permanently. Mars has rocket fuel, potential real estate, and a second chance for civilization.

High seas: Another commons with no sovereignty, freedom of navigation, resource use without ownership. The law of the sea took centuries to develop and is still contested. Mars would need a framework before permanent settlement — after the fact is too late.

The answer: Mars is genuinely new. The combination of: (1) physical impossibility of Earth governance, (2) total dependency on a single corporate provider, (3) potential first-generation Martian-born citizens, (4) possible indigenous life, and (5) strategic resource value — produces a situation that has no historical precedent and no existing legal framework.

The irreversible decisions
🦠 Contamination
Once humans land, Mars is permanently contaminated with Earth microbes. The life-detection question becomes permanently ambiguous. This cannot be undone.
🏗️ First infrastructure
Whoever builds the first permanent habitat defines where the first settlement is. That location becomes the de facto capital of Mars. Its governance structures become the template.
👶 First Martian born
The first human born on Mars is a legal and cultural inflection point. Their citizenship, rights, and identity will be the first test case of whatever governance framework exists — or doesn't.

How to Get There
The physics and engineering of reaching Mars

Getting to Mars is an exercise in patience, orbital mechanics, and the rocket equation. Mars and Earth orbit at different distances from the Sun, so the distance between them varies from 54.6 million km (closest approach) to 401 million km (opposite sides of the Sun). You can't just point at Mars and go — you launch into a Hohmann transfer orbit that puts you on an elliptical path meeting Mars at the right time. This only works efficiently every 26 months, when Earth and Mars are in the right relative positions.

A one-way trip takes approximately 7–9 months. The 2021 missions (Perseverance, Tianwen-1, Hope) took 6–7 months. Faster trajectories are possible but require dramatically more fuel. During the journey, there is no resupply, no rescue, and communications take 3–22 minutes each way — longer as Earth and Mars move apart in their orbits.

The surface of Mars presents its own challenges: thin atmosphere (0.6% Earth pressure) produces severe drag for landing but almost no lift for flight. The sky crane landing system used for Curiosity and Perseverance worked for ~1-tonne rovers. A crewed mission would require landing 10–100 tonnes — a problem that has never been solved at Mars. SpaceX's Starship is the most credible current candidate, using propulsive landing at 52 metres tall and >200 tonnes — roughly 200 times heavier than any previous Mars lander. No one has done this before.

Propellant on Mars — ISRU
The mission that produces its own return fuel

SpaceX's Mars architecture depends on in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) — producing rocket propellant on Mars from local resources, using the Sabatier reaction. CO₂ from the Martian atmosphere + H₂ from water ice = methane + water. The methane is the fuel; the oxygen from splitting water is the oxidizer. Starship runs on liquid methane/liquid oxygen — specifically chosen because these propellants can be produced on Mars.

Perseverance's MOXIE experiment demonstrated oxygen production from Martian CO₂ at small scale (10 grams/hour). A crewed mission return would require tons per day. The difference is seven orders of magnitude. The chemistry is proven. The engineering scale-up is the challenge, and it must be solved and operating on Mars before any crewed mission arrives — otherwise the crew cannot leave.

The hazards
Radiation
Mars has no global magnetosphere and a thin atmosphere. Surface radiation is ~17x Earth background. A 2.5-year round trip mission (6 months each way + 1 year surface) would deliver ~0.66 Sv — approaching NASA's career limits. Shielding during transit and underground habitats on the surface are required.
Global dust storms
Mars has planet-encircling dust storms that can last months. They block 99% of direct sunlight, coat solar panels, and drop temperatures. They killed Opportunity. Any Mars habitat must survive indefinitely without solar power — another argument for nuclear ISRU systems rather than solar-dependent ones.
Thin atmosphere · landing
0.6% of Earth atmospheric pressure: too thin for effective aerobraking alone, not thin enough to ignore. The "seven minutes of terror" — the entry, descent, and landing sequence — must work perfectly every time. No rescue is possible. A crewed EDL failure 350 million km from Earth is a fatal, irreversible event.
Perchlorates
Martian soil contains perchlorates at ~0.5% concentration — toxic to humans if ingested and carcinogenic. They inhibit thyroid function. Any Martian agriculture would require thorough soil decontamination. Perchlorates also have the theoretical advantage of being energy-rich oxidizers — possibly useful for industrial chemistry.
Mars in human history
The red wanderer — 5,000 years of watching, fearing, and naming

Mars has been watched by humans for at least 5,000 years. Its distinctive blood-red color made it unlike anything else in the night sky — brighter than most stars, visibly colored, moving against the fixed stars in a pattern that ancient astronomers tracked with precision. Every major civilization that studied the sky assigned Mars a name, a deity, and a meaning. The name was almost always war. The reason was almost always the color.

Red meant blood. Blood meant battle. Battle meant Mars. This association is so universal — spanning Babylon, Egypt, Greece, Rome, India, China, and Mesoamerica — that it is one of the most consistent acts of human symbolic reasoning across cultures that never communicated with each other. The red wandering star was danger. It was the warrior's planet. It was the god of death in battle and plague in peacetime.

What makes this remarkable is that the association was entirely correct in one sense and entirely wrong in another. Mars is red because of iron oxide — rust — on its surface. There is nothing intrinsically martial about iron oxide. The cultural inference (red = blood = war) was a projection. And yet the projection was so powerful that it named the planet, named the month of March, named Tuesday in most of the world's languages, gave us the words "martial" and "martial arts" and "martial law," named the children Marcus, Martin, and Mark, and defined the astrological meaning of an entire celestial body for three millennia.

The deities
Mars across the world's mythologies
🏛️ Roman — Mars
The most complex Mars deity of antiquity — not simply a god of destruction but a protector of the state, patron of agriculture, and father of Romulus and Remus, the mythological founders of Rome. The Romans ranked Mars second only to Jupiter. Unlike the Greek Ares (reviled by his own parents), Mars was beloved — the guardian father of the Roman people. His month, Martius, was the first month of the original Roman calendar. March is named for him. The Campus Martius — the Field of Mars in Rome — was the center of military and electoral life for centuries.
🏺 Greek — Ares
The most disliked god in the Greek pantheon — reviled by both his parents Zeus and Hera, despised by his sister Athena who called him "a thing of rage, made of evil." Ares represented the chaos and bloodlust of war, not its strategic glory. He had no proper temples in Greece — only battlefields. He fathered the Amazons (with the nymph Harmony) and was associated with Thrace. His affair with Aphrodite — caught in flagrante by Hephaestus's magical net — became one of mythology's great comedies. The Greeks' contempt for Ares is entirely reflected in how they treated the planet: they tracked it with precision but named it after a god they didn't even like.
⚔️ Babylonian — Nergal
The oldest recorded Mars deity. Nergal (also Nirgal) was the Babylonian god of war, plague, fire, and the underworld — the god of "inflicted death." He was also the scorching noonday sun. Nergal stormed the Underworld, deposed its queen Ereshkigal, and became its co-ruler — making him a deity of both the heat above and the darkness below. Babylonian astronomers tracked Mars with extraordinary precision from at least 1500 BCE, recording its retrograde motions and using them to predict war and plague. When Mars moved retrograde, priests issued warnings. The Nergal tradition flowed directly into the Greek Ares and Roman Mars through Hellenistic cultural transmission.
🕉️ Hindu — Mangala / Angaraka
Mars is known in Sanskrit as Mangala (the auspicious one), Angaraka (red like embers or burning coal), and Kuja (the fair one born of earth). He is identified with Kartikeya — the six-headed god of war born from the sparks of Shiva's eyes to slay the demon Taraka. He rides a ram, carries a mace and spear, and has blood-red skin. In Vedic astrology, Mars rules Aries and Scorpio, is exalted in Capricorn, and governs energy, ambition, siblings, accidents, engineers, surgeons, and soldiers. The feared Mangal Dosha — a malefic Mars placement in a horoscope — is believed to cause delayed marriage and conflict. Tuesdays (Mangalavar) are devoted to Mars worship.
🔥 Chinese — 火星 Huǒxīng
In Chinese astronomy, Mars is the Fire Star (火星, Huǒxīng) — one of the Five Planets governing the five elements. Unlike the Western war association, Chinese interpretation was more nuanced: the Fire Star governed fluctuation, change, and the unpredictable force of fire. It was considered inauspicious when it appeared in certain asterisms, signaling floods, war, or the fall of dynasties. Some Chinese sources associated Mars with a purplish tinge rather than red, linking it to royalty and calling it "the purple star." In Japanese (火星, Kasei) and Korean (화성, Hwaseong), the same "Fire Star" name is used.
🏔️ Incan — Mama Ocllo's brother
Inca astronomers tracked Mars as one of the "wandering stars" (huancas) and associated its red color with blood and Inti's warrior aspects. Mars was observed at the horizon and its heliacal rising was used for agricultural timing. The Inca were sophisticated naked-eye astronomers — their calendar combined lunar, solar, and stellar observations. Mars appears in the Inca ceque system of sacred sight-lines radiating from Cuzco. The Mayan equivalent in Mesoamerica also tracked Mars with great precision, recording its synodic period in the Dresden Codex to within a fraction of a day.
🦅 Egyptian — Her Desher / Horus the Red
The Egyptians called Mars Her Desher — "the red one." They connected it to Horus the Red, a battle aspect of the falcon-headed sky god, and sometimes to Set, god of chaos and storms. The red color was not symbolic to them — it was literal blood staining the heavens. Mars in Egyptian astronomical texts appears as a "backward-traveling star" with documented retrograde observations. The planet was also linked to Sekhmet, the lion-headed goddess of war and healing — power that both destroys and cures, just as iron (Mars's metal) makes both weapons and surgical tools.
⚡ Norse — Týr
Tuesday derives not from Mars/Ares but from Týr (Old English: Tiw), the Norse/Germanic one-handed god of law, justice, and single combat — the oldest Germanic sky-father deity. When the Romans encountered Germanic peoples, they identified Týr with Mars (interpretatio romana), so "Tiw's day" became Mars's day in the Romance languages. Týr sacrificed his hand to bind the Fenris wolf — an act of cosmic heroism, not martial aggression. The identification of Týr with Mars tells us something important: the "war god" archetype covers not just violence but honor, courage, law-in-battle, and the sacrifices required to protect cosmic order.
The canals — humanity's greatest astronomical wish
1877–1909 · The civilization that wasn't there

In 1877, Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli observed Mars at opposition and reported canali — channels — across the Martian surface. The word means "channels" in Italian, implying natural features. When translated into English, it became "canals" — implying engineering. Percival Lowell, a wealthy Boston astronomer, ran with the inference.

Between 1894 and his death in 1916, Lowell mapped hundreds of canals at his observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, and published three influential books arguing that Mars was inhabited by an ancient, intelligent civilization — more advanced than ours, desperately engineering a planet-wide canal system to transport water from the poles to the arid equatorial regions as their world died. His Mars was a tragedy: a dying civilization on a cooling, drying planet, building a civilization-scale engineering project to survive.

H.G. Wells read Lowell. In 1898 he published The War of the Worlds — but inverted the tragedy. What if the Martians, desperate for resources on their dying world, came to Earth? The novel launched the entire tradition of Martian invasion literature and set the template for the alien invasion genre. Orson Welles' 1938 radio broadcast of it caused real panic across the American northeast.

The canals were an optical illusion — the human brain connecting random surface features at the limit of resolution into lines. Viking 1 and 2 (1976) ended the dream permanently. But the canal era launched a century of Mars fascination that is the direct cultural precursor to the scientific programs now active on the Martian surface. As Carl Sagan wrote: "Mars has become a kind of mythic arena onto which we have projected our Earthly hopes and fears."

The war gods of the red star
Mars has had a thousand names — nearly all of them mean danger

Every civilization that observed Mars systematically — and that is every major civilization in history — named the red wandering star for something dangerous. War, pestilence, death, destruction, fire. The color was inescapable: Mars shines blood-red, especially near opposition when it is bright in the sky. Ancient peoples associated that color with blood on the battlefield, and built an entire cosmological architecture around the correspondence.

What is striking is not that cultures associated Mars with war — that is nearly universal — but how much the associations diverge beyond that. Nergal was also the god of the underworld, of summer heat that destroys crops, of plague. Ares was despised by the other Greek gods as chaotic and undisciplined. Mars was Rome's second-most important deity, a civilizing force and the father of the Roman people. Mangala was associated with energy, ambition, and the martial arts, but also with property and agriculture. The same red light in the same sky generated radically different meanings — and those meanings shaped centuries of political decision-making, battle timing, marriage counseling, and agricultural planning.

Mesopotamia · 3000 BCE–600 BCE
Nergal — Lord of the Great City of the Dead
The oldest continuous association between Mars and a deity. Nergal was worshipped from ~3000 BCE in Kutha, eventually becoming the king of the Mesopotamian underworld (Kur) and god of war, plague, fire, and the scorching noonday sun. His name translates roughly as "dunghill cock" — a crowing herald of death. In the Late Babylonian astral system, Mars was explicitly identified with Nergal. Babylonian astronomical texts tracked Mars compulsively — each appearance of Nergal in the sky was read for omens. A conjunction with Saturn signified the death of the king. The appearance of Mars at opposition near a major city was a war omen. Babylonian priests sent "astrological reports to Assyrian kings" — a cuneiform archive of political Mars-watching spanning centuries. Nergal also appeared in the Bible — 2 Kings 17:30 records men from Cuthah (Kutha) worshipping him in Samaria.
Greece · ~800 BCE–146 BCE
Ares — Most Hated of the Olympians
The Greeks were unusual in actively despising their war god. In the Iliad, Zeus tells Ares: "To me you are the most hateful of all gods who hold Olympus. Forever quarrelling is dear to your heart." Ares was associated with the brutality, bloodlust, and chaos of war — as opposed to Athena, who represented the strategic, rational aspect of warfare. His children were Phobos (Fear) and Deimos (Dread) — the names now given to Mars's two moons, a tribute to the mythological tradition that the two small, dim companions of the red war god should be named for his sons. Ares was also the lover of Aphrodite (Venus) — the eternal mythological opposition and attraction between War and Love, Mars and Venus, that runs from ancient cosmology through Shakespeare through modern pop psychology. The Greeks named the planet Ares after him — and it was the Romans who renamed it Mars, after their own, more dignified war god.
Rome · 753 BCE–476 CE
Mars — Father of the Roman People
The most important military deity in the Roman pantheon — second only to Jupiter himself. Unlike the despised Ares, Mars was a dignified, even paternal figure. He was the father of Romulus and Remus, the legendary founders of Rome. The month of March — Martius — was named for him and was originally the first month of the Roman year (the Roman year began in spring, when military campaigns resumed). The Campus Martius — the Field of Mars — was the great public space of Rome where armies mustered, elections were held, and athletic contests took place. Augustus built the Temple of Mars Ultor (Mars the Avenger) as the centerpiece of his new forum after avenging Julius Caesar's murder. The word "martial" — martial arts, martial law, martial music — descends directly from Mars's name. So does the name "Marcus," and its variants Mark and Martin.
India · 1500 BCE–present
Mangala / Angaraka / Kuja
Mars is one of the Navagraha — the nine celestial "seizers" whose positions at birth determine destiny in Vedic astrology. Mangala (the auspicious one) rules Aries and Scorpio, governs energy, courage, ambition, and assertiveness. But Mars also carries a severe affliction: Mangal dosha — the condition of Mars in the 1st, 4th, 7th, 8th, or 12th house of a birth chart. Mangal dosha is believed to cause harm to a spouse and is one of the primary considerations in Hindu matrimonial matching — millions of horoscope consultations annually involve determining whether a prospective partner has mangal dosha. Mars is also associated with Lord Kartikeya (Skanda), the god of war, and with the color red, fire, and the metal copper. Tuesday (Mangalvar — Mars's day) is considered auspicious for beginning new ventures related to energy, courage, and competition.
China · ~2000 BCE–present
熒惑 — The Dazzling Deluder
Chinese astronomy tracked Mars under the name 熒惑 (Yíng-huò) — "the Dazzling Deluder" — because its retrograde motion appeared to deceive observers. Chinese court astronomers kept continuous records of Mars's position and behavior, and any anomaly was treated as a political omen. "Yinghuò enters the Heart" — when Mars passed through the Heart asterism (Antares region) — was the most feared event in Chinese astronomical divination, signifying the death of an emperor, the fall of a dynasty, or major military defeat. Emperors who received such an omen would sometimes perform elaborate rituals, redistribute wealth, or even conduct mock abdications to deflect the cosmic threat. The Chinese Mars is also the Fire Planet (火星, Huǒxīng) — the modern Mandarin name — one of the five elements (fire) governing one of the five classical planets.
Egypt · 3000 BCE–30 BCE
Her Desher — "The Red One"
The Egyptians called Mars Heru-khuti ("Horus of the Horizon") in its role as a wandering celestial body, and also Her Desher — simply "the red one." The red color associated Mars with Set, the god of storms, chaos, and desert — and also with Sekhmet, the lion-headed goddess of war and healing, whose fury the gods had to appease with red-colored drinks (beer mixed with ochre) to prevent her from annihilating humanity. Egyptian astronomical records tracked Mars for calendrical purposes; Mars appears in the Ramesside Star Chart and other astronomical ceilings. The association of redness with violence was as instinctive to Egyptian priests as to every other ancient culture.
Mars in Western astrology
The planet of will, drive, and the capacity for violence

In Western astrology — both traditional and modern — Mars is the planet of will, desire, assertion, physical energy, courage, anger, and aggression. Where Venus attracts, Mars pursues. Where the Moon feels, Mars acts. It rules the initiative, the drive to compete, the capacity for both physical exertion and physical violence.

In traditional astrology (Ptolemy, later codified by medieval Arab and European astrologers), Mars was one of the two "malefic" planets — planets inherently associated with difficulty and harm (the other being Saturn). Mars was the "Lesser Malefic," Saturn the "Greater." Mars's malefic qualities expressed as impulsiveness, anger, injury, warfare, accident, and fever. Its benefic qualities expressed as courage, ambition, athletic prowess, and decisiveness. Context determined which.

Mars rules Aries (its primary domicile) and Scorpio (its secondary, though modern astrologers assign Scorpio to Pluto). It is exalted in Capricorn — its most powerful and controlled expression. It is in detriment in Libra and Taurus, and in fall in Cancer. Each natal Mars placement was interpreted as indicating the native's relationship to conflict, desire, physical body, and ambition.

The Mars cycle was also practically important: Mars retrogrades approximately every 26 months, staying in a sign for unusually long periods. Ancient military planners consulted astrologers on Mars retrograde — the conventional wisdom was that campaigns begun during Mars retrograde tended to fail or reverse. This advice influenced actual military decisions in Rome, Persia, and medieval Europe. The planet named for war was consulted before going to war.

The great Mars mistake
How a mistranslation shaped 80 years of human imagination

In 1877, Schiaparelli described faint linear features on Mars as canali — the Italian word for "channels," which can be natural or artificial. When the American astronomer Percival Lowell read about them, he built the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona specifically to study Martian civilization. He spent decades mapping canals, writing books about the dying Martian civilization, and arguing for a complex network of water transportation built by beings far older and more intelligent than humanity.

None of it was real. The canals were an optical illusion — the human brain's pattern-matching tendency connecting random surface features across the vast observational distance. Better telescopes showed nothing. Mariner 4 (1964) photographed craters. The civilization that Lowell had dedicated his career to was never there.

What Lowell's mistake produced, however, was one of the most fertile creative periods in science fiction history — and a deep cultural expectation that Mars might harbor life. That expectation, transmitted through a century of stories, films, and imaginings, is part of why the Cheyava Falls potential biosignature generates global headlines in 2026. We have been hoping for Martian life since 1877. The hope has been wrong about its form — no canals, no civilization, no little green men. But it may not be wrong about the fact.
