History
From feast day to global phenomenon

Saint Patrick was a 5th-century Romano-British Christian missionary who became the foremost patron saint of Ireland. Born around 385 AD, he was kidnapped at 16 by Irish raiders and enslaved for six years before escaping. He returned to Ireland as a bishop and spent decades converting the Irish to Christianity.

The feast day of Saint Patrick — March 17, the traditional date of his death in 461 AD — has been observed by the Catholic Church since the 10th century. For most of its history it was a relatively modest religious observance. In Ireland, pubs were legally required to close on March 17 until 1970.

The transformation into a global celebration began with the Irish diaspora — particularly in the United States, where Irish immigrants used the holiday to maintain cultural identity and build political solidarity. The first recorded St. Patrick's Day parade in America was in Boston in 1737. New York's parade began in 1762, predating American independence.

~461
Death of Saint Patrick. March 17 established as his feast day.
10th c.
Feast day formally entered Irish church calendars. Observed as a religious day of obligation.
1737
First recorded St. Patrick's Day parade in Boston, organized by the Charitable Irish Society.
1762
First New York parade. Irish soldiers in the British Army march through lower Manhattan.
1845–52
Great Famine. One million Irish dead. Two million emigrate. The diaspora that defines the holiday takes shape.
1903
Ireland makes St. Patrick's Day a national holiday. The St. Patrick's Day Act establishes the public holiday.
1962
Chicago dyes the Chicago River green for the first time. A tradition born from a plumber's dye used to detect sewage.
1970
Ireland finally allows pubs to open on St. Patrick's Day — previously they were required to close.
1995
Irish government begins global "greening" campaign — landmark buildings worldwide lit green.
The Shamrock
Why three leaves?

Saint Patrick reputedly used the three-leafed shamrock to explain the Holy Trinity to the Irish. The shamrock became a symbol of Irish identity, worn on the lapel on March 17. Wearing the shamrock is called "wearing of the green."

The four-leaf clover is a separate symbol — luck, not religion. Not traditional to St. Patrick's Day specifically.

The color green
Originally blue

St. Patrick's color was originally azure blue — seen in old Irish coats of arms. Green became associated with the holiday through the Irish nationalist movement in the 18th century, the United Irishmen rebellion of 1798, and the phrase "wearing of the green" — a coded act of defiance during British rule.

Food & Drink · Traditional and diaspora traditions
What people eat and drink

Traditional Irish St. Patrick's Day food is simpler than the diaspora version. In Ireland, the day is still somewhat religious — a Sunday roast or lamb stew is more common than the elaborate American tradition. The "corned beef and cabbage" is almost entirely an Irish-American invention — in Ireland, bacon and cabbage is the traditional dish. Irish immigrants in New York adopted corned beef from their Jewish neighbors because it was cheaper than back bacon.

Irish Soda Bread
Leavened with baking soda, not yeast. Dense, slightly tangy. Sliced and buttered. Truly traditional — eaten year-round in Ireland, not just on March 17.
Bacon & Cabbage
The actual Irish traditional dish — brined back bacon, boiled cabbage, parsley sauce. What corned beef replaced in the diaspora.
Corned Beef & Cabbage
Irish-American tradition, not Irish. Brisket cured in salt brine, boiled with cabbage and potatoes. Became the diaspora standard in New York in the 1800s.
Irish Stew
Lamb or mutton, potatoes, onions, carrots. Simple peasant food that became the comfort food of the diaspora. Lamb is traditional; beef is a substitution.
Colcannon
Mashed potatoes mixed with kale or cabbage. Often has a well of butter in the center. Ancient Irish dish — predates the Famine.
Guinness
Arthur Guinness signed a 9,000-year lease on the St. James's Gate Brewery in Dublin in 1759. The stout is now consumed at approximately 13 million pints on March 17 worldwide.
Irish Whiskey
Jameson, Bushmills, Redbreast. Triple-distilled, typically unpeated. Smoother and lighter than Scotch. Poitín — the illegal home-distilled whiskey — is the ancestral form.
Barmbrack
A yeasted sweet bread with dried fruit. Traditionally baked with objects hidden inside — a ring (marriage), a coin (wealth), a pea (poverty). More associated with Halloween in Ireland but served on feast days.
Note on authenticity
Diaspora vs. homeland

Many St. Patrick's Day "Irish" traditions are Irish-American inventions. Corned beef. Green beer. Leprechauns as a mascot. The modern Irish often find the American celebration unrecognizable.

This is not a criticism — diaspora traditions are authentic traditions. They just belong to a different community than they appear to come from.

Traditions
What happens on the day
🟢
Wearing of the green
Wearing green clothing, shamrocks, or both. In the 18th century, wearing green was a political act — a sign of solidarity with Irish nationalism under British rule. Today it's cultural expression. In some American cities, not wearing green means you get pinched.
🎺
The parade
Parades are the civic heart of the American celebration. They were not originally common in Ireland — the American Irish invented the parade tradition and exported it back. Dublin's major parade only began in 1931.
🍀
Drowning the shamrock
An Irish tradition — at the end of the day, the shamrock worn in your lapel is placed in a glass of whiskey, a toast is drunk, and the shamrock is either swallowed or thrown over the shoulder. The origins are disputed but the practice is documented from at least the 18th century.
💧
Dyeing rivers and fountains green
Chicago dyes the Chicago River green since 1962. The White House fountain has been dyed green. Cities worldwide follow suit. The dye used is environmentally safe (vegetable-based) and fades within hours.
🎶
Traditional music sessions (céilí)
In Ireland, the pub session is the soul of the celebration. Fiddles, tin whistles, uilleann pipes, bodhráns. Tunes are passed orally — many have no written notation. The session is participatory, not performative.
🌍
Global greening
Since 1995, Tourism Ireland has coordinated the "Global Greening" — iconic landmarks lit green on March 17. The Colosseum. The Eiffel Tower. The Great Wall of China. Sydney Opera House. Christ the Redeemer. Niagara Falls.
Etiquette · How to celebrate with awareness
What to know before you go

St. Patrick's Day is celebrated across communities with varying expectations. What's appropriate in South Boston may be different from what's appropriate in Dublin. These are general principles for respectful participation.

✓ Do
Wear green. It's universally welcomed. Shamrocks are appropriate. Four-leaf clovers are fine but are about luck, not St. Patrick specifically.
✓ Do
Learn one Irish phrase. "Lá Fhéile Pádraig Sona Duit" (Happy St. Patrick's Day) or "Sláinte" (to your health, as a toast). Irish people genuinely appreciate the effort.
✓ Do
Understand that the holiday has religious roots. In Ireland, many people attend Mass in the morning before the festivities. Acknowledge that dimension if you're celebrating in an Irish community.
✓ Do
Ask about the local tradition. Every city has its own version. Philadelphians are proud their parade is older than American independence. Bostonians will tell you about Evacuation Day. Chicagoans will explain the river.
✗ Don't
Refer to it as "St. Patty's Day" in Ireland or among Irish people. "Patty" is a woman's name (Patricia). The correct short form is "St. Paddy's Day" — from Pádraig, the Irish form of Patrick.
✗ Don't
Treat leprechauns as the central image of Irish culture. In Ireland, the leprechaun as commonly depicted in American culture is considered a caricature. It originated in folklore but has been commercialized in ways that many Irish find reductive.
✗ Don't
Claim Irish identity you don't have. "I'm Irish on St. Patrick's Day" is a familiar American sentiment — it's harmless in casual celebration but can feel dismissive to people for whom Irish identity carries historical weight including the Famine, colonization, and displacement.
✗ Don't
Assume the celebration is only about drinking. In Ireland the day is still observed religiously by many. In diaspora communities it carries deep meaning about survival, identity, and belonging.
Language note
Irish vs. English

Ireland has two official languages: English and Irish (Gaeilge). The holiday is called "Lá Fhéile Pádraig" in Irish — literally "the day of the feast of Patrick." Saying the Irish name is welcomed, not performative.

"Sláinte" (SLAWN-cha) means "health" and is the standard toast.

Civic Context · Where celebration meets contested civic questions
The civic dimensions of this holiday

St. Patrick's Day has been a site of civic contestation for most of its history in America. It is not politically neutral. Who marches, who is excluded, who is included, and who organizes the parade are civic questions as much as cultural ones.

New York City · Ancient Order of Hibernians
LGBTQ exclusion from the NYC St. Patrick's Day Parade — a 30-year dispute
The Ancient Order of Hibernians, which organizes the NYC parade, excluded LGBTQ groups from marching under their own banners from 1991 until 2015. The exclusion sparked annual protests, political boycotts (Mayor Dinkins boycotted; Mayor de Blasio boycotted and then attended after inclusion). The Supreme Court ruled in 1995 that the parade organizers had a First Amendment right to exclude. In 2015 NBC and Guinness withdrew sponsorship, and the AOH allowed one LGBTQ group to march. The policy has gradually expanded since.

United States · Immigration history
Irish immigration, nativism, and the "No Irish Need Apply" era
Irish immigrants in the 19th century faced systematic discrimination — exclusion from jobs, housing, and political life. "No Irish Need Apply" signs were documented in employment ads. The Know-Nothing Party specifically targeted Irish Catholics as a threat to American democracy. The St. Patrick's Day parade was, for much of the 19th century, a demonstration of Irish civic presence and political power — not just a party.

Ireland · The Troubles
St. Patrick's Day and Irish republican politics in Northern Ireland
During the Troubles (1968–1998), St. Patrick's Day celebrations in Northern Ireland were politically charged. The holiday carried the weight of the national question — whether Northern Ireland should be part of the UK or a united Ireland. Parades were sometimes banned, sometimes flashpoints. The Good Friday Agreement (1998) transformed the holiday's political meaning in the North.

Boston · Political machine
Irish-American political dominance in Boston — legacy, myth, and reality
The Kennedy family, James Michael Curley, the Democratic machine of 20th-century Boston — Irish-American political identity shaped American politics for a century. St. Patrick's Day in Boston is inseparable from this history. The annual St. Patrick's Day breakfast at South Boston's Parish Hall is a major political event where Massachusetts politicians deliver roasts.
